Routing Telemetry, Monitoring, and Anomaly Detection

You cannot enforce what you cannot see, and you cannot see a hijack from a single show bgp summary. This chapter is the telemetry that makes chapters 7, 12, and 19 operational: the global table from outside, your own updates from inside, and alerts that a human can act on. Observability for Networks covers interface counters, logs, and a lab flap drill. Here the object is the routing system.

Learning goals

  • Monitor your prefixes from outside your AS
  • Stream BGP updates and dumps without polling a route processor to death
  • Alert on origin changes and RPKI state changes, and keep statistical detectors in their place
  • Put routing state on a dashboard and in the same queue as other security events

Monitoring the Global Routing Table

Your routers know what they selected. They do not know what the rest of the world selected for your prefixes. External views come from public collectors and looking glasses (RIPE RIS, RouteViews, and operator tools such as bgp.tools). Use them to answer: who is originating my prefix, are more-specifics visible that I did not announce, and what RPKI state do collectors assign?

Poll or stream those views for the prefix list in git, the same list that feeds ROAs. Alert when:

  • The origin AS is not in the expected set
  • A more-specific appears that you did not intend to announce
  • The prefix disappears from all collectors while your edge still thinks it is announced
  • The RPKI state flips from Valid to Invalid or to NotFound

Disappearance from collectors can be your outage, a collector outage, or a leak that overwrote the path in that collector’s view. Treat it as a page and then triage. Do not auto-withdraw because a collector went quiet.

Looking glasses are also how you check a ROA before and after you publish it. The ROV chapter uses that check on purpose. Automate it for your own space.

BGP Telemetry: Streaming Updates and Table Dumps

Polling show bgp from a workstation does not scale and it loads the speaker. Production options:

Feed What you get
BMP (BGP Monitoring Protocol, RFC 7854) A router streams received and selected routes to a collector. This is the right feed for “what did my edge learn?”
MRT dumps Snapshots and update archives from collectors. This is the right feed for “what did the world see?”
BGP logs and session state Up/down, NOTIFICATION codes, max-prefix hits. Necessary and not sufficient
RTR and validator metrics VRP counts, serials, cache connectivity. Chapter 6

Point BMP at a collector that is not one of your route reflectors. The collector holds state so the routers do not have to answer queries during an incident. Store updates long enough to cover the incident-response window in chapter 19 (days, not hours, if you can afford the disk). A hijack that lasted twenty minutes is invisible in a dashboard that only keeps the current table.

Stream validator metrics beside BMP. A VRP-count drop and a wave of new Invalids are the same incident seen from two sides. One graph with both series saves a triage step.

Anomaly Detection: Statistical Methods and ML Approaches

Most routing incidents that matter are rule violations, not subtle statistical outliers. Detect the rules first.

Deterministic alerts, which should be the paging tier:

  • Unexpected origin or more-specific for your prefixes
  • RPKI state change for your prefixes
  • Customer or peer session over the warning threshold for max-prefix
  • Validator cache down, or VRP count diverging between the two implementations
  • Blackhole installed outside the length cap
  • Drift between git and the running policy

Statistical and learning-based detectors, which should be a review queue and not a pager until they earn precision:

  • A peer’s prefix count or update rate far from its baseline
  • A sudden change in path length or in the set of transit neighbours seen for your prefix
  • New more-specifics in the global table for prefixes you do not own, which is someone else’s incident unless they land in your customer cone

Label training data with real incidents and with the churn calendar (maintenance windows, known anycast additions). An unlabeled model of BGP updates will page you for traffic engineering. Keep a human in the loop, and record which alerts were true so the threshold can move. A detector you cannot explain does not belong in the automatic-mitigation path. It can open a ticket.

Route Origin Monitoring and Alerting

Build one alert definition per originated prefix, generated from the same source as the ROA:

prefix 203.0.113.0/24
expected origins: 64500
expected max length: 24
rpki: valid

Page the on-call when a collector reports a different origin, a longer prefix, or a state other than valid. Include in the page: the prefix, the observed origin, the AS path as the collector saw it, the RPKI state, and a link to the runbook in chapter 19. Deduplicate across collectors. Ten collectors seeing one hijack is one incident.

Also alert on the absence of your expected announcement if the prefix is supposed to be visible. A ROA with no announcement is harmless. An announcement you withdrew by mistake is an outage. The expected file should say which case you are in.

For customers you transit, the same logic applies to their contracted prefixes if the contract includes monitoring. You will often see their hijack before they do, because you see the path your other neighbours send. Decide in the contract whether you page them, drop the offending route, or both.

Building a Routing Security Dashboard

One screen, readable during an incident:

  • Session counts by class, and sessions that are not Established
  • Prefixes received versus max-prefix, by neighbour, with the warning line drawn
  • RPKI state mix of the received table: Valid, Invalid, NotFound
  • Cache status and VRP counts for both validators
  • Active blackholes and mitigations, with age
  • Your own prefixes: origin as seen outside, RPKI state, more-specific count
  • Last policy deploy: git revision and time

Keep the screen free of per-update animation. The incident view is counts and a short list of exceptions. A second view can show the raw update stream for the prefix under investigation. Observability for Networks already argues for a small set of signals tied to a question. The question here is “are we originating what we think, and are we accepting what we agreed?”

Integration with SIEM and SOAR Platforms

Routing alerts should land in the same system as the rest of the security queue, with enough structure to be useful:

  • Fields: prefix, length, expected origin, observed origin, peer, as-path, rpki state, git revision, severity
  • The SIEM does not need the full BMP feed. It needs the deterministic alerts and a link to the collector for the rest
  • A SOAR playbook may gather the looking-glass output, the current ROA, and the last deploy diff. It may open the incident template from chapter 19
  • A playbook may not, by itself, withdraw a prefix, publish a ROA, or push a filter. Those are changes with the review path in chapter 17. Automatic mitigation is appropriate only for actions you have already pre-authorised, such as installing an RTBH for a prefix on a list the customer marked as auto-mitigate, with the length cap and the expiry from chapter 14

Clock sync between routers, validators, collectors, and the SIEM is part of the integration. An incident timeline that disagrees by minutes wastes the first half hour.

Summary

  • Watch your prefixes from external collectors, not only from your own RIB
  • BMP and MRT beat polling. Validator VRP counts belong on the same timeline
  • Page on rule violations. Send statistical anomalies to a queue
  • Generate origin alerts from the same list as the ROAs
  • A dashboard shows exceptions, sessions, RPKI mix, and the deployed git revision
  • SOAR may gather context and may not publish a ROA on its own
