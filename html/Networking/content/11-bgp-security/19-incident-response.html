Incident Response: Detecting, Investigating, and Mitigating Routing Attacks

A routing incident is a change in what the world believes, and the clock runs in BGP update intervals. This chapter is a workflow you can follow at 02:00: detect, scope, contain, fix the cause, and tell the neighbours who are still forwarding the bad route. Three case studies reuse shapes from earlier chapters so the workflow has something to bite on.

Learning goals

  • Recognise the early signals of a hijack, a leak, and a bad ROA
  • Pull the same small set of data every time before changing policy
  • Contain with a filter or a withdraw you can undo
  • Escalate to the upstream, the IXP, and the resource holder with evidence attached

Detecting a Routing Incident: Early Warning Signals

Chapter 18’s deterministic alerts are the front door. The signals, and the first hypothesis each one suggests:

Signal First hypothesis
Your prefix seen with a foreign origin Origin hijack or a ROA and announcement that disagree because a scrubber or a new city went live
A more-specific of your prefix, foreign origin More-specific hijack. Longest match is already moving traffic
Your prefix seen with your origin and an unexpected upstream Leak, or a new transit you have not recorded
Your prefix RPKI Invalid while you still announce it Your ROA is wrong, expired, or narrower than the announcement
Customer session hits max-prefix That customer is leaking a table at you
VRP count for one RIR collapses Validator or repository problem, not a hijack
Your prefix vanishes from collectors You withdrew it, or a widespread filter now drops it

Open an incident when a signal hits your own space or a customer’s contracted space. Do not wait for a user ticket. Traffic may already be wrong while your own routers still look healthy, because the bad route is preferred somewhere else.

Investigation Workflow: Tools, Queries, and Data Sources

Work in one order so containment does not destroy the evidence.

  1. Write down the time you saw the alert and the prefix, origin, and path in the alert.
  2. External view. Check a collector or looking glass for that prefix: all origins, all more-specifics, AS paths, RPKI state. Save the output into the incident record.
  3. Your edge. BMP or show bgp for the prefix: did you learn the bad route, from which neighbour, with what local preference and RPKI state? Did you re-advertise it?
  4. Authorisation. Compare against git: the originated-prefix list, the ROA you intended, the customer’s allow-list, the last deploy time.
  5. Validator. Confirm the VRP your cache actually has for the prefix. A looking glass and your cache can disagree when one of them is stale.
  6. Decide the class before you touch policy: hijack, leak, bad ROA, capacity, or validator outage. The case studies below are those classes. The wrong contain-step makes the incident worse. Withdrawing your own Valid route during a leak you do not originate is one example.

Keep a query list in the runbook so the on-call does not invent flags under pressure. The list is short: the prefix on a looking glass, the prefix in your RIB, the VRP, the neighbour’s received-prefix count, and the git revision on the edge.

Case Study: Responding to a Prefix Hijack

Shape. Collectors show 203.0.113.0/24 originated by an AS that is not yours, or they show 203.0.113.0/25 originated by that AS while you announce only the /24. This is the YouTube shape from chapter 3, with you as the victim.

Contain.

  • If you learned the bad route, reject it from that neighbour now, from the reviewed emergency path, and open the git commit that makes the reject permanent
  • If you did not learn it, your local filter cannot pull it back. Announce the covering prefix and, if you must win longest-match immediately, announce the more-specifics you are willing to ROA. Publishing the ROA for those more-specifics comes before or with the announcement, or enforcing networks will drop your recovery
  • Ask the neighbour that is propagating the hijack, usually the attacker’s transit, to filter their customer. A route you cannot see locally dies when that transit withdraws it. That is how the 2008 incident actually ended
  • If the prefix is NotFound because you never published a ROA, publish a tight one as part of the follow-up. It will not rewind the last hour. It changes the next one

Do not. Raise local preference on a route you do not have. Call it a DDoS and blackhole your own aggregate. Disable ROV globally because one route looks wrong.

Case Study: Diagnosing a Route Leak

Shape. A customer session goes from 40 prefixes to tens of thousands, or collectors show your AS in the path of prefixes you do not originate, sitting between two of your transits. ROV says Valid. The origin is innocent.

Contain.

  • The customer session: it should already have hit max-prefix. If it has not, the limit was too high. Shut the session or apply the emergency reject, then put the limit back to the contracted number in git
  • Confirm you stopped re-advertising the leaked table. Egress tags are how you check. If from-customer was applied to a full table, every transit received it
  • Tell your transits you leaked, what the window was, and that you have contained it. They may be filtering you by then. Silence looks like the leak is ongoing
  • If the leak is someone else’s and you are only a victim, drop the offending neighbour’s unexpected prefixes if they beat your normal paths, and rely on their upstream to fix the source. Keep ROV on. It is correctly saying Valid

Follow-up. Add the path-filter or the OTC role that would have rejected the valley, and add a test in the generator for “customer sends a path containing our transit AS.”

Case Study: Invalid ROA Incident Response

Shape. Your own prefix flips to RPKI Invalid on collectors. You are still announcing it. Either the ROA origin is not the AS you are using, the length exceeds maxLength, the ROA expired, or a parent publication failed and a different covering ROA now contradicts you.

Contain.

  • Decide whether the announcement or the ROA is the intended state. The git list is the authority. If the announcement is right, fix the ROA. If the ROA is right, withdraw the rogue more-specific
  • Fixing a ROA through a hosted portal is a publish and a wait for caches to refresh. Have the validator view, not the portal’s green check alone, confirm the new VRP
  • If a delegated CA stopped renewing, restore publication before objects expire worldwide. This is a chapter 20 failure showing up as an incident
  • If you dropped a customer because their ROA is wrong and their service is down, you may temporarily accept that prefix with a SLURM or a local permit while they fix the ROA. The exception has an expiry the same day. Leaving it in place is how Invalid becomes permanent policy

Do not delete all your ROAs to “stop the bleeding.” Networks that were dropping Invalid will then treat you as NotFound and reach you, and you have also removed the protection that made the hijack class detectable. Fix the specific object.

Communication, Coordination, and Escalation

Routing incidents cross organisations by definition. Write the contact path before you need it.

  • Your transits and your IXP have a NOC contact and a security contact. Store them next to the neighbour inventory
  • The offending AS has a contact in the IRR aut-num and in PeeringDB or the RIR record. Use it after you have the path in writing
  • Resource holders for a prefix you do not own get a notice when you are the upstream who can see the leak. Include prefix, path, timestamps, and what you have done
  • Internal comms: one incident lead, one person changing policy, one person updating the record. Three people editing route-maps is three new incidents
  • Customer comms for a managed prefix: what failed, what you changed, what they will see, and when you will revert a temporary accept
  • Afterward, a short review: which alert fired, which did not, whether the generator test would have caught it, and the commit that fixed the class. Feed the true positive back to chapter 18

Regulatory or contractual reporting, where you have it, uses the same timeline. Do not keep a second unofficial story.

Summary

  • Alerts open the incident. The class decides the containment
  • Save the external view and your RIB before you change policy
  • Hijacks need the propagating upstream to withdraw, and they need your ROA to be tight
  • Leaks need the max-prefix and the export tag. ROV will say Valid
  • A bad ROA is fixed by editing the specific object, not by abandoning RPKI
  • Contacts, a single changer, and a committed follow-up turn a 02:00 page into a shorter incident next time
