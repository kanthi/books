BGP Communities and Route Attribute Security

Communities are the policy API customers and peers actually use. They are also unauthenticated transitive attributes. This chapter is how to offer that API without letting a neighbour blackhole your backbone. The well-known communities and a small FRR lab live in BGP no_export and Well-Known Communities.

Learning goals

  • Tell standard, extended, and large communities apart
  • Publish a community catalogue that is the only set your route-maps honour
  • Strip everything else on the way in and on the way out
  • Treat a scrubbing-centre or blackhole community as a privilege with a prefix-length check

BGP Communities: Standard, Extended, and Large Communities

Kind Shape RFC Use
Standard 32 bits, usually written ASN:value with a 16-bit ASN RFC 1997 Well-known values and older provider communities. Breaks for 32-bit ASNs
Extended Typed, used heavily for VPN route targets and for some traffic-engineering signals RFC 4360 Easy to accept by accident if a route-map matches too broadly
Large 32-bit ASN plus two 32-bit values, written ASN:X:Y RFC 8092 The community format that fits modern ASNs. Prefer it for new policy

Well-known standard values still cross the Internet:

  • no-export — do not advertise to eBGP peers
  • no-advertise — do not advertise to any peer
  • no-export-subconfed — stay inside the local confederation sub-AS
  • BLACKHOLE (65535:666, RFC 7999) — discard this more-specific
  • graceful-shutdown (65535:0, RFC 8326) — prefer another path, this peer is leaving

Because these are transitive, a neighbour can attach no-export to a route you then fail to propagate, which looks like a leak in reverse: your customers never see a prefix you accepted. Decide explicitly whether you honour well-known communities from each neighbour class. Honouring no-export from a customer is normal. Honouring it from the entire transit table means one upstream can suppress your onward advertisement.

Using Communities for Policy Enforcement

A maintainable design gives every action a community you document, and the route-map only matches those.

Typical catalogue entries:

  • Customer sets you:100:1 to request a higher local preference inside your AS
  • Customer sets you:100:9 to request prepending toward peers
  • Customer sets you:666:ASN or the RFC 7999 blackhole community to request discard of a more-specific under attack
  • You set internal communities on ingress (from-customer, from-peer, from-transit) and you match those on egress so a peer route is never exported to another peer

The internal ingress tag is the control. Egress route-maps match your tag, not the neighbour’s story. Policy & Filters uses local preference directly in a lab. In production the customer-facing knob is a community, and the local preference is written by your map, so the customer never sends LOCAL_PREF itself.

Document the catalogue in the same repository as the route-maps. An undocumented community that one engineer used during an incident will still be in the config in a year.

Security Implications of Community-Based Routing

Communities are not signed. Any network on the path can add, remove, or rewrite them, subject only to the filters those networks bothered to write. Consequences:

  • A community that raises local preference is a traffic-steering privilege. If it survives from a peer session onto your decision process, the peer outranks your transits
  • A blackhole community accepted for any prefix length, from any neighbour, is a remote packet drop for arbitrary destinations. Chapter 14 constrains it
  • A community that triggers no-export or a long prepend is a reachability and traffic-engineering action. Scope it to the customer’s own prefixes
  • Info communities that you add for telemetry (learned-at-ixp-1) leak topology if you forget to strip them on egress. Stripping is the default. Adding a public looking-glass community is a deliberate publication

The secure posture is deny by default. Strip all communities on ingress, then accept the small set in the catalogue, and only from neighbour classes allowed to use that row. Strip internal communities on egress.

Community Manipulation: Threats and Defenses

Threat Defense
Customer attaches your internal from-transit tag and becomes eligible for re-export Strip on ingress before you add your own tag. Never accept your internal range from outside
Peer sends RFC 7999 BLACKHOLE for a prefix they do not own Accept blackhole requests only for prefixes that neighbour is allowed to originate, and only at the agreed length (usually /32 or /128, sometimes /24 or longer)
On-path network strips the community you needed for traffic engineering Do not depend on a community surviving someone else’s AS. Set policy at your edge from your own state
Typo in a match value triggers blackhole for a wide aggregate Test route-maps on a stored table before commit. Prefer large communities with your ASN in the first field so the number is obviously yours
Well-known no-advertise arrives on a transit route Strip well-known communities from transit unless you have a written reason to honour one of them

GRACEFUL_SHUTDOWN is the example of a well-known community worth honouring from peers: it asks you to pick another path before they go down. Pair it with a local preference change that is reversible, and confirm the other path exists or you will blackhole when you obey.

Vendor-Specific Community Implementations

Vendors agree on the three formats and disagree on everything around them.

  • Default behaviour differs on whether well-known communities are honoured, rewritten, or stripped
  • Extended-community types used for Layer-3 VPNs (route targets) must not be accepted from the public Internet onto a VPN customer. Internet edge and VPN edge are different policy chains
  • Some platforms send communities only when a knob is on, so a design tested on FRR silently does nothing on another OS until you enable send-community
  • Large-community support is what you require for a 32-bit ASN. A platform that only does 16-bit standard communities will mangle the catalogue
  • Informational communities in the 65535: range are reserved. Using them for local policy collides with the next well-known value the IETF defines

Chapter 16 names the knob per platform. The requirement is the same everywhere: an explicit send list, an explicit accept list, and a strip of the rest. The no-export lab is the FRR reference for the well-known subset.

Automating Community-Based Policies

Generate the route-map from the catalogue, do not maintain the catalogue in the route-map. A small table of (community, neighbour class, action, constraints) should produce the ingress maps. Constraints include “prefix must be inside the neighbour’s allowed set” and “length must be at least /24.” The generator refuses a catalogue row that sets local preference from a peer or a transit.

Review the generated map in git. A human-readable catalogue page belongs in the same change as the diff. Tests for the generator are table tests: given a route with community X from neighbour class Y, the result is accept, local-pref 120, or reject. Chapter 17 fits this generator next to the IRR and ROA filter build. One pipeline, one review, one rollback.

During an incident, adding a community by hand on a router creates drift the next deploy will wipe, or worse, a deploy that will not wipe it because someone excluded the router. The incident procedure in chapter 19 uses the same catalogue and a committed exception with an expiry.

Summary

  • Standard, extended, and large communities are unsigned policy signals
  • Publish a catalogue. Strip everything else on ingress and strip internal tags on egress
  • Blackhole and local-preference communities are privileges, bound to the neighbour’s own prefixes
  • Well-known communities such as no-export and BLACKHOLE need an explicit honour-or-strip decision
  • Generate maps from the catalogue and test them as data
