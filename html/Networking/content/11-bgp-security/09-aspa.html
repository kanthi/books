ASPA: Autonomous System Provider Authorization

ASPA is the RPKI object that says “these AS numbers are my providers.” It exists so a leak, which ROV scores as Valid, can be scored as a relationship failure. As of October 2026 the profile and the verification procedure are still IETF Internet-Drafts, objects are being published, and router enforcement is ahead of you only if your release notes say so.

Learning goals

  • State the leak shape ASPA is built to detect
  • Read an ASPA as a customer AS plus a provider set
  • Apply the valley check at the level of “up, across, down”
  • Decide what to do while most AS paths still contain networks with no ASPA

The Problem ASPA Solves: Relationship Validation

ROAs bind an origin to a prefix. A leaked route keeps the legitimate origin and adds a hop that the origin’s provider never agreed to. The YouTube incident was an origin problem. A customer re-advertising a full table to a second transit is a relationship problem. ASPA targets the second.

The relationship it encodes is directional and small. The customer signs the list of providers. Providers do not sign the list of customers. Verification asks whether the AS path is consistent with those signed lists: traffic should go up toward providers and then down toward customers, with at most one peer-to-peer step in between. A path that goes down and then up again is the leak shape from chapter 1.

ASPA does not sign the hop-by-hop path. It does not stop a provider from announcing the customer’s prefix to the wrong peer if the provider is on the signed list. It does not replace export filters. It gives everyone else a signed hint they can check without seeing the contract.

ASPA Data Model: Provider Authorization Records

An ASPA names one customer AS and a set of provider AS numbers. The customer is the holder of the AS number in the RPKI, so the signature follows the same allocation chain as a ROA. Publishing “AS 65001’s providers are 65010 and 65011” means those two may appear as the upstream when 65001 is the customer side of a hop.

Operational contents of a correct record:

  • Every network you take a full table or a default from, including backup transits
  • No peers, unless you have a reason the verification draft treats as a provider relationship. Listing a peer as a provider widens the paths that look legal
  • An update when you add or drop a transit, done before the BGP change, not after the leak alarm
  • A deliberate empty or minimal set only when you truly have no providers (you are the top of a cone). Getting this wrong marks legitimate paths as leaks

RIPE NCC enabled ASPA creation in its hosted RPKI in December 2025. ARIN followed in January 2026. A March 2026 measurement of early deployment found on the order of 1,600 adopting ASes, concentrated where the portal made it easy, and reported commitments from all five RIRs to support creation by the end of 2026. By 29 October 2026 a public ASPA checker was loading a few thousand ASPA records. That is real deployment and thin coverage. Most AS paths will contain at least one AS with no record.

ASPA Cryptographic Structure and RPKI Integration

An ASPA is a signed object under the end-entity certificate for the customer AS, published in the same repository as ROAs, listed on the same manifest, and fetched by the same relying party. Validators that understand the object emit validated ASPA payloads. Routers receive them over an RTR version that carries them. Routinator can serve that payload on RTR v2. A cache or a router that stops at RTR v1 will validate origins and ignore ASPA without a loud failure. Check the session version.

Revocation and expiry behave like other RPKI objects. A lapsed ASPA does not fall back to “this AS has no providers” in a useful way. It falls back to “no statement,” which verification treats as unknown. Unknown is the dominant state in 2026. Policy that drops unknown will drop most of the Internet.

AS-Path Validation with ASPA

Verification walks the AS path using the provider sets. Two algorithms show up in the drafts and in operator tools, and they apply to different neighbours:

  • A path received from a customer is checked so the customer is not exporting their providers’ routes to you (the leak you would then amplify).
  • A path received from a provider or a peer is checked so you are not importing a leaked path from upstream.

The walk looks for an up-ramp (customer toward provider) and a down-ramp (provider toward customer). A valley, up after down, is invalid. Missing ASPA objects along the path yield unknown, not invalid. Invalid means the objects that do exist contradict the path.

Forwarding policy in 2026, if your OS implements the check at all:

Result Policy that matches the coverage
Valid Accept, subject to your other filters
Invalid Drop on sessions where you have watched the volume and you can reach the neighbour to fix their ASPA
Unknown Accept. Count it. Do not drop it while records are sparse

This is the same discipline as NotFound for ROAs, for the same reason.

Deployment Status and Timeline

What is true late in 2026:

  • The specification is an Internet-Draft pair (the ASPA profile and the verification procedure), not yet an RFC
  • Hosted creation works at RIPE NCC and ARIN, with other RIRs at different stages
  • Relying parties, including Routinator and rpki-client, can emit ASPA payloads
  • Router support in widely deployed commercial releases is the lagging piece. Open-source BGP implementations have experimental or partial support. Treat “ASPA” in a datasheet as a question for the release note and for a lab, not as an enabled feature
  • Measurement work through early 2026 found sparse coverage and said large-ISP enforcement is more likely to follow general availability than to precede it

RFC 9234 roles and the OTC attribute are the related mechanism you can turn on sooner, on the sessions whose vendors implement roles. OTC is hop-by-hop and unsigned. ASPA is signed and path-wide. They stack. Neither removes the customer prefix filter.

Limitations and Remaining Gaps

  • Sparse objects mean most paths stay unknown. ASPA will not catch a leak that only traverses networks without records
  • A customer who lists the entire world as providers makes every path look legal. Bad ASPAs are a new data-quality problem, smaller than IRR and still real
  • Provider-to-provider leaks at the top of the graph, where both sides have empty or unusual provider sets, need careful reading of the verification rules. Do not assume “Invalid” in a tool matches your router’s code
  • ASPA does not pin the prefix. A path can be ASPA-valid and RPKI-invalid, or the reverse. Keep both checks
  • An on-path attacker who is a listed provider is inside the signature. ASPA constrains shape, not honesty of a legitimate upstream
  • Forged AS paths that still respect the signed provider sets pass. BGPsec is the design that binds hops cryptographically, and chapter 10 explains why you do not have it in production

Publish ASPA records for your own AS now if your RIR portal allows it, even before you enforce. Records have to exist before other people’s enforcement can help you, and the same chicken-and-egg was how ROAs spread.

Summary

  • ASPA signs “these are my providers” so valley-shaped leaks can be marked invalid
  • Unknown is the common result until coverage improves. Dropping unknown is an outage
  • The object rides the same RPKI repositories and needs RTR and router support that ROV does not
  • In 2026, publish records, monitor results, and keep prefix filters and OTC
  • A listed provider can still leak you. ASPA is a relationship check
