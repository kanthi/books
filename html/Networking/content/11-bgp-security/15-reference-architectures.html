Reference Architectures for Production Environments

The controls in the previous chapters combine differently for a transit provider, a dual-homed enterprise, a content network, a cloud fabric, an IXP route server, and a managed edge. This chapter is those combinations. It names roles and policy, not vendor syntax. Chapter 16 maps the same roles onto specific stacks.

Learning goals

  • Pick the architecture that matches who pays whom
  • Place ROV, filters, and max-prefix on the sessions each architecture actually has
  • Say what the network announces, and what it refuses to re-announce
  • Identify the single session that, if left open, turns this design into a leak

Transit Provider Architecture: Tier 1, Tier 2, and Regional ISPs

A transit provider has customers, peers, and sometimes upstreams of its own. The cone is the product.

Session Import Export Limits
Customer Allow-list plus ROV Invalid drop, path matches the customer cone, tag from-customer Full table, a partial, or default, as sold Max-prefix near the allow-list
Peer Cone allow-list, ROV, tag from-peer Your cone only Max-prefix near the cone size
Upstream, if you are not tier-1 Bogon and length filter, ROV, tag from-transit Your cone only Max-prefix above the global table, with headroom

Route reflectors carry customer and peer routes inside the AS. They do not get a private copy of import policy. The eBGP edge is the only place a route becomes eligible for a tag. Reflection then distributes the tagged route. A reflector that peers externally is an edge router and must run edge policy.

Tier-1 networks have no transit sessions and a large peer set. Their hard problem is peer filters at scale, which means generated cones and a human process when a peer disputes the cone. Regional ISPs have fewer peers and a greater temptation to accept a full table from a peer “temporarily.” The template should make that temptation a new neighbour of class transit, with transit export rules, so you do not export that full table onward.

ROAs for the provider’s own aggregates, ASPA listing the upstreams, and OTC or BGP roles on customer sessions are part of this architecture even when some customers will never publish anything. The provider still drops Invalid so a customer hijack is not re-exported as Valid-looking reachability. A customer route that is NotFound is a business question. Many providers accept it and encourage a ROA. That matches chapter 7.

Enterprise Multi-Homing with Dual Transit

The enterprise buys transit from two providers, originates a small set of prefixes, and does not provide transit to anyone. Edge Dual-Home Lab is this design in Containerlab: two eBGP uplinks, prefix filters, and failover.

Production additions on top of that lab:

  • ROAs for every prefix you announce, maxLength equal to the announced length, published before you depend on the second provider
  • The same prefix list outbound to both providers. Asymmetric announcements are deliberate traffic engineering, not an accident of a missing stanza on one side
  • Inbound: accept default, or a full table if you actually use it. Drop Invalid if you take a full table. If you take only default, ROV on that session does almost nothing and your providers’ ROV is what protects your prefixes
  • Local preference prefers one provider until it fails. A community from the provider, not a hand-set MED you do not control, should be how you ask them to prefer a link
  • No customer sessions. If a lab or a subsidiary sits behind you, give it a real customer policy or you will re-advertise whatever it sends
  • Max-prefix on each transit, sized for a full table if you take one, so a provider session that goes wrong resets instead of filling the branch router’s memory

The failure you are designing for is one provider withdrawing, not for a hijack of someone else’s prefix. Keep the design that simple and the ROA correct, and you have done the enterprise’s share of routing security.

SaaS and Content Provider Architectures

Content networks announce the same prefixes in many cities and use the Internet’s preference for nearby exits. The security shape:

  • One origin AS for a given prefix in the ROA, or an explicit set of origins if anycast is done from more than one AS. Unlisted cities become Invalid the day you turn them up
  • Tight aggregates. More-specifics for traffic engineering each need a ROA or a maxLength you have justified in a ticket. Prefer a ROA per announced prefix
  • No transit for customers. Export is your own list only. A CDN that also offers “bring your own IP” has become a transit provider for those prefixes and needs the customer row of the table above, including the customer’s ROA
  • Scrubber integration from chapter 14, including a ROA for the scrubber’s origin before the first redirect
  • Anycast means a hijack of the prefix in one region is a hijack of the service in that region. Monitoring has to be per-city, not only “does the prefix exist somewhere.”

Cloud and Data Center Routing Security

Inside a fabric, BGP is often the underlay and the overlay control plane (EVPN). The trust problem is smaller and sharper: every speaker is yours, until a tenant or a bare-metal customer brings a router.

  • Fabric sessions use a private ASN or an internal ASN and do not export to the Internet. Leakage is an export filter on the data-centre edge, default reject, allow the public prefixes the site is supposed to originate
  • Tenant BGP, if you offer it, is a customer session. The tenant’s prefixes are the allow-list. The tenant does not get to set fabric-wide local preference except through a community you defined
  • ROV on the fabric underlay is usually pointless because you are not taking the global table there. ROV on the edge router that speaks to transit is the same as the enterprise or provider case
  • A route reflector or a route server for tenants is a shared control plane. Isolate its management plane. A tenant who can open TCP 179 to the reflector and is missing an allow-list is a leak inside your building
  • The fabric chapters in this book (Clos, FRR fabric) are the forwarding design. They do not replace an edge policy at the point the fabric touches a transit provider

IXP Route Server Security

A route server concentrates multilateral peering. Members send routes to the route server and receive other members’ routes, subject to the route server’s policy. The route server is in the control plane and usually not in the forwarding path. Its security failures affect many networks at once.

A responsible route-server configuration:

  • One policy per member: accept that member’s cone only, with max-prefix, with ROV, and with a path that starts with the member’s AS
  • Do not accept transit-sized tables from a member who is registered as a peer
  • Filter bogons and RPKI Invalid before redistribution
  • Honour a published blackhole community with the length and ownership checks from chapter 14
  • Offer BGP roles or OTC if the implementation supports them
  • Publish the filtering rules. Members cannot comply with a secret as-set name
  • Isolate the route-server host. It holds a session with every member. Control-plane protection on that host is the IXP’s version of chapter 13
  • Log rejected prefixes with a reason, and let the member see their own rejects. A silent drop is how a member “temporarily” removes their filters on a bilateral session and re-creates the leak beside the route server

Members still run bilateral sessions around the route server. Those sessions need the member’s own peer policy. The route server is not a substitute for it.

Managed Service Provider Architectures

A managed provider operates the edge for a customer who does not run BGP. The trust boundary is awkward because the managed provider’s engineers can change policy for many customers, and the customer’s address space may still be registered to the customer.

  • ROAs live in the holder’s RIR account. The contract should say who is allowed to publish them. A managed provider originating space it cannot sign will produce Invalid routes as soon as the customer, or a previous contractor, publishes a different origin
  • Separate BGP daemons or separate routing instances per customer where the platform allows. A shared route-map with a customer-id community is one typo away from exporting customer A to customer B’s transit
  • Change control from chapter 17 is the product. Customers buy the inability of one engineer to edit a live router without a reviewed commit
  • Monitoring is per customer prefix. The provider’s own NOC runs the dashboard from chapter 18, and the customer gets the origin-change alert for their space
  • Offboarding withdraws announcements and removes the origin from any ROA the provider created, in the same change window. Leaving a ROA behind that names the provider’s AS makes the customer’s next provider Invalid

Summary

  • Providers filter customers and peers tightly and export only their cone
  • Enterprises announce a small ROA-backed list to two transits and do not re-advertise what they learn
  • Content networks treat every city and every scrubber origin as a ROA change
  • Fabrics keep tenant BGP on the customer side of an allow-list and do not leak the underlay
  • Route servers enforce per-member cones. Managed edges keep ROAs and route-maps in the holder’s name, under reviewed change
