Blackholing and Remote-Triggered Blackholing

Blackholing throws traffic away on purpose, as close to the source as you can arrange. It is a reachability tool that looks exactly like an attack when the prefix or the community is wrong. This chapter is the mechanics, the RFC 7999 community, and the checks that keep a scrubbing action from discarding a customer aggregate.

Learning goals

  • Describe a blackhole route as a more-specific whose next hop is discard
  • Trigger that route from a customer community without accepting the community for arbitrary prefixes
  • Connect RTBH to a DDoS scrubber without making the scrubber a second Internet edge
  • Recover on a timer and a listed prefix, not by hoping someone remembers

Blackhole Routing: Basic Mechanics

A blackhole route is a prefix whose next hop is a discard interface (a null interface, a discard route, or a hardware equivalent). Longest-match forwarding sends traffic for that prefix into the discard and leaves the covering prefix alone. That is why blackholes are almost always more-specifics. A blackhole for the covering aggregate is an outage for everything inside it.

Inside one AS you can install the discard route by configuration. That only saves your own links. Attack traffic has already crossed your transit to reach you. Remote-triggered blackholing asks an upstream, or an IXP route server, to install the discard closer to the senders.

The route is still a BGP route. It needs an origin, a community or a next hop the upstream recognises, and a filter on their side. You are asking someone else to drop packets destined to an address you claim to hold. Their acceptance policy is the security control. Yours is the request policy.

Remote-Triggered Blackholing: Design and Operation

RFC 3882 describes the pattern. You announce the attacked more-specific to a provider session that is configured for blackhole announcements. The provider recognises the signal, sets the next hop to discard, and does not export that route to the rest of the world as a normal path. Traffic for that more-specific dies at the provider. Traffic for the rest of your prefix keeps flowing.

Design rules that keep the mechanism in its box:

  • Use a dedicated session, or a dedicated policy on the existing session, so blackhole announcements cannot be confused with your ordinary cone
  • Accept and send only prefixes inside the customer’s own allocated space
  • Cap the length. A common contract is IPv4 /32 and IPv6 /128, or “longer than or equal to /24 inside your aggregates.” Write the one you mean
  • Cap the count. A customer under attack needs a handful of hosts, not a de-aggregation of their /16
  • Tag the route so your monitoring can list every active blackhole and its age
  • Prefer a community you document over an informal “set next hop to 192.0.2.1” that only one engineer remembers. Next-hop signalling and community signalling both appear in production. Pick one per provider and test it

IXP route servers often offer a blackhole community for members. The same rules apply, with the extra hazard that a mistake is visible to every member who honours the community.

Standard RTBH: Discard Communities and Null Routes

RFC 7999 reserves the standard community 65535:666 (BLACKHOLE). A provider that honours it should discard traffic to the tagged prefix and should not read the tag as permission to accept the prefix in the first place. The prefix filter still runs. The community is the action, not the authorisation.

On your edge the implementation is a route-map:

  • Match the neighbour and the community
  • Match the prefix against that neighbour’s allowed space and the length cap
  • Set next hop to discard, or propagate the community to the upstream that will discard
  • Reject the combination for any other neighbour or any other length

A null route on a router discards on that router only. Propagating the community discards on every router that honours it. Know which of the two you configured. Operators have “mitigated” an attack by null-routing only inside one city while the attack arrived in another, and they have also discarded a prefix globally when they meant to discard it on one link.

Do not attach BLACKHOLE to a route you also announce as reachable to someone else without understanding which announcement wins. A more-specific blackhole and a covering reachable prefix are the usual pair. Two announcements of the same prefix, one with the community and one without, depend on the receiver’s policy.

Per-Prefix RTBH and Next-Hop Based Blackholing

Two signals are common, and providers document which one they implement.

Community-based. You announce the prefix with 65535:666 or the provider’s own large community. Their policy matches the community and discards. This travels through route servers that forward communities and do not rewrite next hops the way you hoped.

Next-hop based. You announce the prefix with a next hop the provider has published as their discard address, often a documentation address dedicated to that purpose. Their policy matches the next hop. This fails closed if an intermediate speaker rewrites next hops, which eBGP does by default. Use it on the direct session the provider told you to use, and test after every OS upgrade that rewrites next hops.

Per-prefix is the normal scope. A customer asks for one attacked address. You do not offer a community that blackholes “whatever this customer currently announces.” Bind the request to the prefix in the same UPDATE, and re-check authorisation on every UPDATE. A session that remembers “this neighbour is allowed to blackhole” and then accepts a new prefix is the accident chapter 11 warned about.

Some providers support a rewrite so the blackhole is installed only inside their AS (no-export added by them). Confirm they add it. If they export your /32 blackhole as a normal route, the rest of the Internet may send that destination to them and no further, which can be what you want for an attack and is a surprise for a prefix you still wanted reachable elsewhere.

Integration with DDoS Mitigation Systems

A scrubber or a mitigation appliance changes the picture. The goal becomes “send the attacked prefix to the scrubber, then return the clean traffic,” which is traffic engineering, not discard. RTBH remains the right tool when the volume is so large that you would rather drop it at the upstream than pay to carry it to the scrubber.

Integration patterns that stay reviewable:

  • The mitigation system may inject a blackhole or a redirect only through the same generator and the same community catalogue as a human. A direct unauthenticated BGP session from the appliance into your edge, with its own policy, is a second control plane
  • Redirects use a dedicated next hop or a dedicated community that sets local preference toward the scrubber, and they match the same prefix authorisation as blackholes
  • The appliance withdraws the more-specific when the attack ends. If the appliance dies, the more-specific must disappear with it (a session reset withdraws it) or you are stuck sinking traffic into a dead box
  • Log every injection with prefix, length, requester, and expiry. Chapter 18’s routing dashboard should show active mitigations next to origin alerts so an operator can tell a scrubber from a hijack

A scrubber that originates your prefix from the scrubber’s AS needs a ROA. Otherwise every network that drops Invalid will blackhole you as a side effect of the mitigation. Add the scrubber origin to the ROA before the first redirect, and remove it when the contract ends.

Safety and Recovery: Avoiding Accidental Blackholes

Accidental blackholes are configuration, not fate.

  • Default reject on the blackhole policy. No match means no discard
  • Refuse aggregates. If the allowed length is /32, a /24 does not match “more specific than the customer aggregate” in a loose regex sense. Test the boundary
  • Refuse prefixes the neighbour is not authorised to originate, even if the ROA is NotFound and someone is in a hurry
  • Put an expiry on every blackhole. A weekly job withdraws anything older than the window unless a ticket extends it
  • Make the withdraw path a single committed change that removes the community and the more-specific. Do not leave a static null route behind that BGP withdrawal will not touch
  • Page when a blackhole is installed for a prefix shorter than the cap, or for a prefix outside the neighbour’s allow-list. That page means the filter failed
  • Practice the withdraw in the lab the same way you practice the install. The edge dual-home lab is the place for failover. A blackhole drill belongs beside it: announce a documentation /32, watch the discard counter, withdraw, watch traffic return

Recovery of a mistake is a BGP withdraw plus a check that no router still has a static discard for that prefix. Look in the forwarding table, not only in the BGP table. A leftover static null route does not disappear when the session resets.

Summary

  • A blackhole is a more-specific pointed at discard
  • RTBH asks an upstream to install that discard, using a community or a dedicated next hop
  • RFC 7999’s BLACKHOLE community is the action. The prefix filter is the authorisation
  • Scrubbers redirect. They need a ROA if they originate, and they need a session whose death withdraws the route
  • Length caps, allow-lists, and expiry are what keep a mitigation from becoming the incident
