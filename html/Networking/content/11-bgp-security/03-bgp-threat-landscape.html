The BGP Threat Landscape

Every attack in this chapter is an UPDATE, a session event, or an attribute that your policy honours. Naming them separately matters because origin validation stops only one row of the table below.

Learning goals

  • Classify an incident as an origin hijack, a more-specific, a leak, or a path edit before picking a control
  • Connect resource exhaustion and session resets to router protection, not to RPKI
  • Walk one historical hijack hop by hop, including how it was stopped

Prefix Hijacking: Origin Attacks and Traffic Redirection

An origin hijack announces a prefix with an AS path whose rightmost AS is not the holder. Equal-length announcements compete in the decision process. A more-specific hijack announces a longer prefix. Longest-match forwarding prefers it everywhere the more-specific is accepted, which is why it is the reliable way to redirect traffic.

The hijacker does not need to complete a TCP handshake with the victim. They need one neighbour willing to accept and re-advertise the prefix. That neighbour is usually a transit provider with a loose customer policy. The rest of the Internet is behaving as specified.

What origin validation changes: if a ROA covers the prefix for the legitimate origin and the length, the hijack is RPKI Invalid at every network that drops Invalid. What it does not change: space with no ROA stays NotFound and is still accepted; a ROA with a wide maxLength makes the more-specific Valid for the attacker’s origin if that origin is the one in the ROA, or Invalid if it is not. Forged origin plus loose maxLength is the case RPKI ROAs and the maxLength Trap demonstrates.

A hijacker who only wants to observe traffic must also get the packets out again (a “backhole” versus a blackhole). Many incidents never bother. The traffic simply dies at the announcer, which is enough to deny the service.

Route Leaks: Accidental and Intentional Violation of Routing Policy

RFC 7908 defines a route leak as an announcement that violates the sender’s intended relationship with the receiver. The common accidental form: a customer learns a full table from one transit and re-advertises it to another transit, and that second provider treats it as customer routes, tags it with a high local preference, and exports it to the world. Origins are correct. ROV says Valid. Preference is wrong, so a large fraction of the Internet moves.

Intentional leaks are the same BGP. The difference is motive. Controls that care about motive will not fire. Controls that care about valley-free shape will.

Deployed defences, in the order operators actually turn them on:

  • Per-neighbour prefix filters, tight for customers and peers, sanity-capped for transit
  • AS-path filters so a customer path is one AS long, or matches a known downstream cone
  • BGP roles and the OTC attribute (RFC 9234) on implementations that negotiate them
  • ASPA, where both the objects and the router code exist (chapter 9)
  • Max-prefix, so a customer who suddenly sends 900,000 routes resets or stops, instead of becoming a transit provider

AS-Path Manipulation: Injection, Shortening, and Splicing

The neighbour builds the AS path you see. They can prepend their own AS to lengthen a path, which is normal traffic engineering. They can also:

  • Shorten by stripping hops, which makes a worse path look better and can hide the true origin
  • Splice by inserting a foreign AS, including the legitimate origin, so the path looks plausible and ROV still returns Valid if the rightmost AS matches a ROA
  • Poison by inserting a particular AS so that AS drops the route as a loop

Path-length games beat networks that prefer “shortest path” and have no ROV. Origin forgery that preserves the legitimate rightmost AS beats ROV. Neither is theoretical. Both are ordinary UPDATE construction. BGPsec (chapter 10) is the design that signs the hop list. It is not the control you have on a production edge in 2026. Relationship filters limit how much of this you are willing to accept from a given neighbour.

Maximal Prefix Announcements and Exhaustion Attacks

A session that will accept an unbounded number of prefixes will accept a de-aggregated copy of the global table, or a pile of more-specifics inside the customer’s own allocation. Memory and CPU on the receiving router are the targets. The control is max-prefix with a number taken from the contract, plus a warning threshold below it. Chapter 13 covers the failure mode where the limit itself becomes the outage because the default action is to disable the session.

A second form is announcement of thousands of more-specifics that are RPKI Valid because maxLength allows them. The ROA is doing what it was configured to do. The router still has to carry the routes. Length limits in filters and max-prefix both still apply after ROV.

DoS Against the Routing Control Plane

The control plane is a general-purpose CPU. Packets aimed at it (TTL-expired floods, packets to the router’s own addresses, TCP 179 from the whole Internet) compete with BGP keepalives. Control-Plane Protection is the lab for policing that path. For BGP specifically:

  • Accept TCP 179 only from configured neighbours
  • Use GTSM on single-hop eBGP so packets with an unexpected TTL never reach the daemon
  • Rate-limit unreachables and exceptions
  • Keep management in a separate plane from customer traffic

A router that is too busy to send keepalives withdraws its table when neighbours hit the hold timer. The data plane may still be forwarding on stale hardware entries for a while. The symptom operators see is “the network flapped,” not “the CPU was policed.”

BGP Session Hijacking and TCP Exploitation

BGP runs in a long-lived TCP session. Off-path injection of a RST, or of a fake UPDATE, requires guessing sequence numbers or being on path. TCP-AO or MD5 raises that bar. They do not tell you the neighbour is the AS you think it is if you pointed the session at the wrong address. On-path attackers who already sit between two peers see the session in the clear unless the authentication option is on, and even then they see the routing information if they are a legitimate hop.

Operational reality in 2026: many sessions still use MD5 because TCP-AO support is uneven, and many single-hop sessions rely on GTSM plus source-address filtering and call that enough. Record which sessions have neither. Those are the ones a shared IXP LAN, or a compromised customer router, can talk to if your ACL is wide.

Malformed Updates, Route Flapping, and Churn Attacks

A peer that withdraws and re-announces a prefix every few seconds burns CPU on every router downstream that accepted it. Route flap dampening (RFC 2439) was the protocol answer. Revised guidance (RFC 7196) and operator experience pushed many networks to turn it off, because legitimate bursts after a failure were suppressed for too long. Chapter 13 takes that trade-off. Until then, the practical controls are max-prefix, per-neighbour prefix limits inside their allocation, and not accepting more-specifics below an agreed length.

Malformed attribute handling is the other churn tool. RFC 7606’s treat-as-withdraw is the behaviour you want. Test it when you upgrade. A release note that “improves BGP error handling” is a security change.

Attribute Manipulation: MED, Local Preference, Communities

Lever Who can pull it Effect if you trust it from the wrong neighbour
MED The adjacent AS, if you compare it You prefer their expensive or hostile exit
Local preference Anyone you forgot to strip it from They outrank every transit path in your AS
Communities you honour Anyone who can send the value They trigger blackhole, prepend, or no-export on your behalf
Next hop The peer You forward into a sink, or fail to resolve

eBGP does not accept LOCAL_PREF from the wire in a conforming implementation. The bug is usually a route-map that copies a community into local preference. That map is the security boundary. Review it as one.

Real-World Incident Anatomy: A Detailed Case Study

YouTube, 24 February 2008. The pieces, in causal order:

  1. YouTube announced its service prefixes, including a covering prefix, from its own AS.
  2. Pakistan Telecom was ordered to block YouTube inside Pakistan and did it by announcing a more-specific of YouTube’s space (a /24 inside YouTube’s block) into BGP, with the intent that the route stay national.
  3. PTCL’s upstream, PCCW, accepted the customer announcement and propagated it. Once the more-specific was in the global table, longest-match sent a large share of YouTube’s traffic toward Pakistan, where it was discarded.
  4. YouTube’s counter-announcement of even longer prefixes pulled traffic back where those prefixes were accepted. That is a forwarding win, not a policy win.
  5. PCCW withdrew the leaked route and filtered the customer. The incident ended when the neighbour that had global reach stopped exporting it.

Read the incident as an acceptance failure at one provider boundary, multiplied by longest-match. ROV, had it been deployed and had YouTube published a tight ROA, would have marked PTCL’s origin Invalid. A customer prefix-filter at PCCW that listed only PTCL’s own allocations would have stopped it without ROV. Both controls are doing different jobs, and the incident needed only one of them at the right hop. Chapter 19 uses the same shape as a response exercise and adds the leak and the bad-ROA cases, which this anatomy does not cover.

Summary

  • More-specific origin hijacks win on longest match. ROV stops them when the ROA is tight and enforcement is on
  • Leaks are Valid origins on the wrong path. Filters, roles, OTC, and ASPA are the controls
  • The neighbour is the author of the AS path you see
  • Unbounded acceptance is a resource attack. Max-prefix is the circuit breaker
  • The 2008 YouTube event is an upstream that exported a customer’s more-specific
