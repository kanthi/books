Route Stability Controls: Dampening, Max-Prefix, and Limits

Filters decide whether a prefix is allowed. Limits decide whether the neighbour may consume the control plane. This chapter is dampening, max-prefix, update rate, and per-neighbour quotas, and the outages those knobs cause when they are set as if they were free.

Learning goals

  • Explain what flap dampening suppresses, and why many networks turned it off
  • Set max-prefix so a leak trips it and a normal failover does not
  • Put a quota on a customer without putting the same quota on a transit full table
  • Choose stability or responsiveness on purpose for each neighbour class

BGP Route Dampening: Mechanism, Parameters, and Trade-offs

Route flap dampening (RFC 2439) assigns a penalty every time a prefix is withdrawn and re-announced. The penalty decays exponentially with a configurable half-life. Above a suppress threshold the prefix is not re-advertised. Below a reuse threshold it is eligible again. A maximum suppress time caps the punishment.

The parameters exist because early BGP speakers collapsed under flapping prefixes. The side effect, documented in the revised recommendations (RFC 7196), is that a prefix which flaps during a real failure is hidden from the Internet after it has recovered. Reachability stays broken while the penalty decays. Many large networks disabled dampening and absorbed the CPU cost with faster hardware and with better error handling.

If you enable it anyway:

  • Use the RFC 7196 figures as the starting point, not the ancient defaults some stacks still ship
  • Do not dampen your own customers’ prefixes so hard that their failover is invisible to you
  • Never dampen on the basis of attribute changes you yourself cause
  • Monitor the suppressed-route count. A non-zero steady state means you are hiding reachability

Prefer max-prefix and prefix-length caps to stop a neighbour from injecting flap, and leave dampening off unless you have a measured CPU problem and a dashboard for suppressed routes.

Max-Prefix Limits: Protecting Router Resources

maximum-prefix caps how many prefixes a neighbour may send. Past the cap, the usual action is to disable the session and optionally restart it after a timer. That action protects the router and takes down every prefix from that neighbour, including the ones you wanted.

Set the cap from data:

Neighbour Starting point
Single-homed customer A small multiple of the contracted prefix count, so one planned de-aggregate fits and a full table does not
Peer A small multiple of the generated cone size, plus headroom you reviewed
Transit Above today’s global table for that address family, with headroom for growth, and reviewed on a schedule
Route server The member’s own cap, never the full table, unless the route server is your chosen path to a full table and you meant that

Use a warning threshold (commonly around 80 percent) that pages without resetting. Treat the page as a filter bug or a real incident, not as noise. A warning you ignore trains you to miss the reset that follows.

Separate IPv4 and IPv6 limits. A single combined counter surprises people when one family is attacked and the other family is what you needed for management.

Know the action on your OS. “Warning only” protects nothing and is the right first week. “Restart after 30 minutes” can flap in a loop if the neighbour still sends the flood. “Idle until a human clears it” is the right action for a customer leak once you trust the threshold. Write the action down in the neighbour template.

Rate Limiting BGP Updates

Max-prefix caps the steady state. It does not cap how fast the peer gets there. A neighbour can still send a burst that spikes your CPU inside the cap. Platforms expose different tools: update groups, input queue limits, per-peer update rate, and TCP window behaviour. The principle is the same. Bound the work one session can impose, and prefer dropping or delaying that session’s updates over delaying keepalives for every session.

Apply the strictest rate limits to neighbours with the least privilege. A customer who sends a thousand updates a second is misconfigured or hostile. A transit provider re-sending a large cone after a session reset is doing what you asked. Size the transit limit for session re-establishment, or the limit will extend the outage.

Route servers at an IXP concentrate many members’ updates. A per-member cap at the route server protects the other members. Chapter 15 includes that in the route-server pattern.

Per-Peer and Per-Customer Quotas

Quotas are the contract turned into numbers: prefix count, maximum length, number of more-specifics inside each aggregate, and sometimes a bound on AS-path length. Store them next to the prefix list. The generator fails the build if the list exceeds the quota, so you find the problem before the session resets.

A customer who buys a burst above the quota gets a committed change to the quota, not a logged-in engineer raising max-prefix until the alarm stops. The second habit is how the cap becomes the size of the global table.

Per-peer quotas for peers are the cone size. Recompute them when you regenerate filters. A quota that was right when the peer had 40 prefixes is a self-inflicted outage when they acquire a customer with 400 and your ROA intersection correctly wants to allow them.

Tuning for Stability vs Responsiveness

Stability and responsiveness pull apart in three places.

Timers. Short hold times detect a dead peer faster and reset the session on a brief loss. Longer hold times ride through control-plane pauses. Single-hop eBGP with BFD, covered in BFD — Fast Failure Detection, is the right tool when you need fast failover without shortening the BGP hold timer to the point that a CPU spike looks like a dead peer. BFD tells you the forwarding path died. The hold timer tells you the BGP speaker died. They are different signals.

Convergence features. Graceful restart and long-lived graceful restart keep forwarding across a speaker restart. They also keep forwarding on stale paths while a hijack is withdrawn. Know whether a neighbour is allowed to stale-route, and for how long. A stale Valid route is still a route.

Enforcement delay. ROV and filter changes can apply immediately or at the next policy run. Immediate is what you want when you are dropping a hijacked more-specific. Immediate is also how a bad ROA publish blackholes you everywhere at once. Your own ROA changes want a staged publish. Your inbound drop of someone else’s Invalid can be immediate.

Write the choice down per neighbour class. Customers: fast BFD, tight max-prefix, no dampening. Transits: default hold timers, max-prefix with headroom, no dampening, graceful restart only if you accept stale forwarding. Route reflectors: protect them with control-plane policing, because every client pays for their CPU.

Summary

  • Dampening hides recovered prefixes. Leave it off unless you measure a need and watch the suppressed count
  • Max-prefix is a circuit breaker sized from the contract or the table size, with a warning below the reset
  • Rate limits belong on low-privilege sessions. Transit resets are bursts you have to absorb
  • Quotas live in the generator, not in an engineer’s shell history
  • BFD, hold timers, and graceful restart answer three different failure clocks
