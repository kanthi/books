The Lonely Runner Conjecture

After reading this chapter, you will be able to state the Lonely Runner Conjecture in both its circular track and view-obstruction formulations, prove why the isolation threshold of \(1/(k+1)\) is sharp, implement exact rational grid searches using Python’s fractions module to find loneliness witnesses, and analyze the structural arithmetic bottlenecks that make a general proof one of the most stubborn open challenges in Diophantine approximation.

Problem Statement and Relative Speeds

Consider \(k+1\) runners on a circular track of unit length \(1\). Suppose they all start at the same origin at time \(t = 0\) and run in the same direction at constant, pairwise distinct speeds. A runner is declared lonely at time \(t\) if the distance along the track between that runner and every other runner is at least \(1/(k+1)\). The Lonely Runner Conjecture asserts that every runner will eventually become lonely.

Because the physics of the system depends only on relative displacements, we can choose any runner as our reference frame. Fix runner \(0\) with speed \(s_0\), and shift all coordinates so that runner \(0\) remains stationary at the origin for all \(t \ge 0\). The relative speeds of the remaining \(k\) runners become:

\[ v_i = s_i - s_0 \quad (i = 1, \dots, k). \]

Since the original speeds were distinct, no \(v_i\) is zero. Distance on the circle ignores direction (\(\|-x\| = \|x\|\)), so a negative \(v_i\) can be replaced by \(|v_i|\). If the speeds are rational, multiplying them all by a common denominator only rescales time. Irrational speeds need a real argument, but it is a known reduction that the conjecture for integer speeds implies it for all real speeds. From here on, each \(v_i\) is a positive integer.

Let \(\|x\|\) denote the distance from a real number \(x\) to the nearest integer:

\[ \|x\| = \min_{m \in \mathbb{Z}} |x - m| = \min\big(x - \lfloor x \rfloor, \, 1 - (x - \lfloor x \rfloor)\big). \]

On a circle of circumference \(1\), the distance from a runner with relative speed \(v_i\) to the stationary runner at the origin at time \(t\) is exactly \(\|t v_i\|\). Runner \(0\) becomes lonely at time \(t\) if and only if every other runner is at distance at least \(1/(k+1)\) from the origin:

\[ \|t v_i\| \ge \frac{1}{k+1} \quad \text{for all } i \in \{1, \dots, k\}. \]

Thus, the conjecture has the following standard Diophantine formulation:

Tip

The Lonely Runner Conjecture (Moving-Runner Form). For any \(k\) distinct positive integers \(v_1, v_2, \dots, v_k\), there exists a real time \(t > 0\) such that: \[\|t v_i\| \ge \frac{1}{k+1} \quad \text{for all } i = 1, \dots, k.\]

Here \(k\) represents the number of non-stationary runners. The total number of runners on the track is \(n = k + 1\).

Tightness and the Consecutive Speed Case

The fraction \(1/(k+1)\) is not arbitrary. It is the absolute maximum isolation distance one can guarantee. If the conjecture holds, the constant \(1/(k+1)\) cannot be replaced by any larger number \(1/(k+1) + \epsilon\).

To see why this bound is tight, consider the consecutive speed spectrum:

\[ (v_1, v_2, \dots, v_k) = (1, 2, \dots, k). \]

At any arbitrary time \(t > 0\), examine the positions of the reference runner and the \(k\) moving runners on the circle \([0, 1) \cong \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}\). These positions are:

\[ 0, \, t \pmod 1, \, 2t \pmod 1, \, \dots, \, kt \pmod 1. \]

The claim is that at least one of these \(k\) moving runners is always within \(1/(k+1)\) of the origin. This is Dirichlet’s approximation theorem: for any real \(t\),

\[ \min_{1 \le j \le k} \|j t\| \le \frac{1}{k+1}. \]

Proof. Look at the \(k + 2\) numbers \(0, \{t\}, \{2t\}, \dots, \{kt\}, 1\) in \([0, 1]\), where \(\{x\}\) is the fractional part. Split \([0, 1]\) into \(k + 1\) closed intervals of length \(1/(k+1)\). By the pigeonhole principle, two of the \(k + 2\) numbers fall in the same interval, so they differ by at most \(1/(k+1)\). Those two numbers are \(\{at\}\) and \(\{bt\}\) for some \(0 \le a < b \le k\), after replacing the endpoint \(1\) by \(\{0 \cdot t\} + 1\). Their difference is within an integer of \((b - a)t\), so \(\|(b - a)t\| \le 1/(k+1)\) with \(1 \le b - a \le k\). \(\square\)

Equality is attained, for example, at \(t = 1/(k+1)\). There the \(k\) runners occupy the exact positions:

\[ \frac{1}{k+1}, \, \frac{2}{k+1}, \, \dots, \, \frac{k}{k+1}. \]

In this configuration, every runner is at distance at least \(1/(k+1)\) from the origin, with runners \(1\) and \(k\) attaining the distance of exactly \(1/(k+1)\). By Dirichlet, no time pushes all of them strictly farther than \(1/(k+1)\) from zero, so the constant \(1/(k+1)\) cannot be improved. Speed sets where the best possible separation is exactly \(1/(k+1)\) are called tight. The consecutive speeds are the standard example, and there are others, such as \((1, 3, 4, 7)\) for \(k = 4\), which appears in the script below.

History and Proved Cases

Jörg M. Wills first posed the conjecture in the late 1960s in the context of Diophantine approximation. In 1973, T. W. Cusick independently formulated it as a view-obstruction problem in geometry. The name “Lonely Runner Conjecture” comes from the 1998 paper of Bienia, Goddyn, Gvozdjak, Sebő and Tarsi, which linked it to nowhere-zero flows in graphs.

Progress has come one runner at a time. In the table, \(n = k + 1\) is the total number of runners.

The case \(k = 1\) is trivial: runner \(1\) with speed \(v_1\) reaches the opposite point of the circle at time \(t = 1/(2v_1)\), where its distance to the origin is exactly \(1/2\).

The case \(k = 2\) (three runners) asks for a time \(t\) where both \(\|t v_1\| \ge 1/3\) and \(\|t v_2\| \ge 1/3\). It can be settled by elementary modular arithmetic and interval analysis.

For the seven-runner case, Barajas and Serra needed a long, intricate combinatorial argument. That case stayed the frontier for about 17 years.

The recent progress goes through a reduction by Terence Tao (2017): if the conjecture holds for all integer speeds up to an explicit bound (depending on \(k\)), it holds in general. That turns each case into a finite, though astronomically large, check. In September 2025, Matthieu Rosenfeld proved the eight-runner case. He showed by computer that the product of the speeds in any counterexample must be divisible by many specific primes. That forces the product above a Tao-style size bound that a minimal counterexample must stay under. Weeks later, Oxford undergraduate Tanupat Trakulthongchai sharpened the method with a sieve and proved the nine- and ten-runner cases, which appeared in the Electronic Journal of Combinatorics in June 2026. Rosenfeld independently proved nine runners a few days later. Both authors expect that eleven runners will need a new idea, and the general conjecture remains open.

The View-Obstruction Geometry

Cusick’s 1973 formulation provides a visual and geometric dual to the circular track. Instead of thinking about points wrapping around a 1D circle over time, we can view the lonely runner problem as a straight ray passing through a lattice of obstacles in \(k\)-dimensional Euclidean space \(\mathbb{R}^k\).

Let \(\mathbb{R}^k\) have standard coordinates \((x_1, x_2, \dots, x_k)\). At every point with half-integer coordinates:

\[ \mathbf{c} \in \left(\mathbb{Z} + \frac{1}{2}\right)^k, \]

place an open \(k\)-dimensional hypercube with side length \(2\lambda\), aligned with the coordinate axes. That is, each obstacle is an open box of the form:

\[ C(\mathbf{c}, \lambda) = \left\{ \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^k : \|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{c}\|_\infty < \lambda \right\}. \]

Now, consider an observer standing at the origin \(\mathbf{0} = (0, \dots, 0)\) looking into the positive orthant \(\mathbb{R}_{>0}^k\). A ray emanating from the origin in direction \(\mathbf{v} = (v_1, v_2, \dots, v_k)\) is parameterized by:

\[ \mathbf{r}(t) = t \mathbf{v} = (t v_1, t v_2, \dots, t v_k), \quad t > 0. \]

Does this ray avoid hitting any of the open cubes, or is the observer’s line of sight obstructed?

Cusick’s form of the conjecture says that every such ray hits an obstacle once the cubes are large enough. Take closed cubes \([\frac{1}{k+1}, \frac{k}{k+1}]^k + \mathbb{Z}^k\), centered at the half-integer points with side \(\frac{k-1}{k+1}\). The ray \(t\mathbf{v}\) meets one of them exactly when every coordinate satisfies \(\{t v_i\} \in [\frac{1}{k+1}, \frac{k}{k+1}]\), which is \(\|t v_i\| \ge \frac{1}{k+1}\) for all \(i\). So:

  • The ray hits an obstacle if and only if runner 0 is lonely at some time \(t\).
  • The conjecture says that no ray with positive integer direction can see through this forest of cubes forever.
  • Shrinking the cubes even slightly lets the ray in direction \((1, 2, \dots, k)\) slip through. That is the geometric form of tightness.

Why the Conjecture Is Hard

If the lonely runner conjecture is so simple to state, why does it resist a general proof? Several mathematical obstacles converge in this problem:

  1. Local vs. Asymptotic Behavior. Classical ergodic theory and Diophantine approximation (such as Weyl’s criterion or Kronecker’s theorem) describe the asymptotic distribution of lines on a torus as \(t \to \infty\). They prove that the trajectory \(\{t \mathbf{v}\}_{t \ge 0}\) is dense in \(\mathbb{T}^k\) if the speeds are linearly independent over \(\mathbb{Q}\). But the lonely runner conjecture is strictly non-asymptotic: it requires finding a witness time \(t\) in a bounded, concrete interval (specifically \(t \in [0, 1]\)), for any integer vector \(\mathbf{v}\), including highly degenerate vectors with strong linear dependencies.

  2. Arithmetic Resonances. When the speeds \(v_i\) share common factors or form structured patterns (such as geometric progressions \(1, 2, 4, 8, \dots\) or dense arithmetic progressions), their positions on the circle do not behave like independent random variables. Instead, they exhibit phase locking: runners cluster together, creating recurring intervals where at least one runner repeatedly sweeps past the origin.

  3. Curse of Dimensionality. The “lonely region” where all \(\|t v_i\| \ge 1/(k+1)\) is the complement of a union of periodic intervals:

    \[ E = [0, 1) \setminus \bigcup_{i=1}^k \bigcup_{m=0}^{v_i} \left( \frac{m - \frac{1}{k+1}}{v_i}, \, \frac{m + \frac{1}{k+1}}{v_i} \right). \]

    As \(k\) grows, the number of intervals being removed from \([0, 1)\) scales proportionally to \(\sum_{i=1}^k v_i\). Showing that this union does not cover the entire interval \([0, 1)\) requires understanding subtle overlaps between intervals whose frequencies \(v_i\) are coprime.

  4. Many disguises. The conjecture is closely tied to graph theory. It is linked to nowhere-zero flows (Bienia et al.) and to bounds on the circular chromatic number of distance graphs. Results proved in one setting often do not transfer cleanly to another.

Rational Time and Candidate Discretization

The search for a witness time \(t\) begins over the continuous real domain \(t \in \mathbb{R}_{>0}\). However, continuous searches are ill-suited for exact computation. Fortunately, continuous time can be reduced to rational candidates.

The isolation function:

\[ F(t) = \min_{1 \le i \le k} \|t v_i\| \]

is continuous, periodic with period \(1\) (since \(v_i \in \mathbb{Z}\)), and piecewise linear. On any interval where no runner crosses an integer or half-integer, \(F(t)\) is linear with slope \(\pm v_i\) for some active runner \(i\). The local maxima of such a function must occur at the critical points: 1. Peaks of individual runners, where \(\|t v_i\| = 1/2\), occurring at \(t = (2m+1)/(2v_i)\). 2. Equidistant collisions between two runners, where \(\|t v_i\| = \|t v_j\|\). This occurs when \(t v_i \pm t v_j = m \in \mathbb{Z}\), which implies:

\[ t = \frac{m}{v_i + v_j} \quad \text{or} \quad t = \frac{m}{|v_i - v_j|}. \]

Because all critical points are rational numbers, if a lonely time exists, the maximum gap is attained at a rational number.

This gives an exact finite test for any fixed speed set: evaluate \(F\) at every critical point \(m/(2v_i)\), \(m/(v_i + v_j)\) and \(m/|v_i - v_j|\) in \((0, 1)\), and take the maximum. Exercise 3 below builds an equivalent interval-based solver.

The scripts in this chapter use something simpler: a uniform rational grid

\[ t = \frac{a}{N}, \quad a \in \{1, 2, \dots, N-1\}, \]

with \(N = (k+1) \cdot \max(v) \cdot c\) for a small integer factor \(c\). A grid point is always an exact lower bound on \(\max F\). Because \(t = 1/(k+1)\) is on every such grid, the consecutive-speed witness is always found.

Note

Search Principle. A discrete rational grid search can find witnesses that prove loneliness; it cannot disprove the conjecture. If a discrete grid finds a point \(t = a/N\) where \(\min_i \|t v_i\| \ge 1/(k+1)\), that point is an exact certificate. If the grid fails, the continuous peak may simply lie between grid points.

Finding Witnesses: Exact Discrete Search

To perform exact calculations without floating-point rounding errors, we use Python’s standard library fractions.Fraction. The script lonely.py implements the distance function \(\|x\|\) and searches over rational multiples \(t = a/N\).

"""Lonely runner conjecture: find witness times on a discrete rational grid."""
from fractions import Fraction


def dist_to_int(f: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Distance from rational number f to the nearest integer, ||f||."""
    rem = Fraction(f.numerator % f.denominator, f.denominator)
    return rem if rem <= Fraction(1, 2) else 1 - rem


def best_gap(speeds: tuple[int, ...], N: int) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction]:
    """Find the grid point t = a / N maximizing min_i ||t * v_i||.

    Returns (maximum_gap, witness_time).
    """
    best_g = Fraction(0, 1)
    best_t = Fraction(0, 1)
    for a in range(1, N):
        t = Fraction(a, N)
        gap = min(dist_to_int(t * v) for v in speeds)
        if gap > best_g:
            best_g = gap
            best_t = t
    return best_g, best_t


def main() -> None:
    cases = [
        (1, 2, 3),
        (1, 3, 4, 7),
        (1, 2, 3, 4, 5),
    ]
    for speeds in cases:
        k = len(speeds)
        target = Fraction(1, k + 1)
        # Choose grid resolution proportional to (k + 1) * max(speeds)
        N = (k + 1) * max(speeds) * 24
        gap, witness = best_gap(speeds, N)
        print(
            f"speeds={speeds} (k={k}) -> "
            f"target: {target}, best_gap: {gap}, witness_t: {witness}"
        )


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

Running python3 lonely.py evaluates the gap on each test case and prints the target bound, best gap, and witness time.

speeds=(1, 2, 3, 4, 5) (k=5) -> target: 1/6, best_gap: 1/6, witness_t: 1/6

In the output, each case reaches its threshold \(1/(k+1)\) exactly, at \(t = 1/(k+1)\). For the consecutive speeds \((1, 2, 3)\) and \((1, 2, 3, 4, 5)\), the Dirichlet argument shows that no time does better, so the grid has found the true maximum. \((1, 3, 4, 7)\) is also tight. Evaluating \(F\) at its full critical-point set gives a maximum of exactly \(1/5\), so it is a genuine non-consecutive tight instance and not a grid artifact.

Exhaustive Verification for Triples

For \(k=3\) non-stationary runners (four runners total), the isolation threshold is \(1/(3+1) = 1/4\). We can systematically test all speed triples \(1 \le v_1 < v_2 < v_3 \le 12\) to confirm that every triple admits a rational witness with gap at least \(1/4\).

The script exhaustive.py tests all \(\binom{12}{3} = 220\) triples.

"""Exhaustive check of speed triples for the lonely runner conjecture."""
from fractions import Fraction
import itertools


def dist_to_int(f: Fraction) -> Fraction:
    """Distance from rational number f to the nearest integer, ||f||."""
    rem = Fraction(f.numerator % f.denominator, f.denominator)
    return rem if rem <= Fraction(1, 2) else 1 - rem


def best_gap(speeds: tuple[int, ...], N: int) -> tuple[Fraction, Fraction]:
    """Find the grid point t = a / N maximizing min_i ||t * v_i||."""
    best_g = Fraction(0, 1)
    best_t = Fraction(0, 1)
    for a in range(1, N):
        t = Fraction(a, N)
        gap = min(dist_to_int(t * v) for v in speeds)
        if gap > best_g:
            best_g = gap
            best_t = t
    return best_g, best_t


def main() -> None:
    k = 3
    target = Fraction(1, k + 1)
    tight_cases = []
    checked = 0

    triples = list(itertools.combinations(range(1, 13), 3))
    for speeds in triples:
        checked += 1
        N = (k + 1) * speeds[-1] * 12
        gap, witness = best_gap(speeds, N)
        assert gap >= target, f"Conjecture violated for {speeds}: gap {gap} < {target}"
        if gap == target:
            tight_cases.append((speeds, witness))

    print(f"Checked {checked} speed triples with 1 <= v1 < v2 < v3 <= 12.")
    print(f"All triples have a grid witness gap >= {target}.")
    print(f"Triples where the best gap is exactly {target} (tight cases):")
    for sp, wit in tight_cases:
        print(f"  speeds: {sp}, witness t: {wit}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

In the output, all 220 triples reach the threshold \(1/4\). The best gap is exactly \(1/4\) only for \((1, 2, 3)\), \((2, 4, 6)\), \((3, 6, 9)\) and \((4, 8, 12)\), which are all integer multiples of \((1, 2, 3)\). This list is exact, not just a grid estimate:

  • A grid value is a lower bound on the true maximum. So every other triple, where the grid already beats \(1/4\), is truly non-tight.
  • The four listed triples are scalings of \((1, 2, 3)\), so their maximum is exactly \(1/4\).

Pitfalls and Common Mistakes

When analyzing or implementing the Lonely Runner Conjecture, engineers frequently encounter the following subtle traps:

  1. Confusing Runner Counts (\(k\) vs. \(k+1\)). Literature inconsistently switches between \(n\) total runners and \(k\) relative speeds. If there are \(7\) total runners on the track, fixing one runner leaves \(k = 6\) non-zero relative speeds, and the required distance is \(1/7 = 1/(k+1)\). Applying the bound \(1/6\) to \(6\) relative speeds is incorrect and changes a solvable problem into a false statement.

  2. Floating-Point Imprecision in Boundary Checks. Testing \(\|t v_i\| \ge 1/(k+1)\) using standard IEEE 754 floating-point numbers (float) will produce subtle bugs. Fractions such as \(1/3\), \(1/5\), and \(1/7\) have infinite repeating expansions in binary. A calculated distance of 0.19999999999999998 will register as strictly less than 0.2, falsely rejecting a valid witness time. Always use exact arithmetic (fractions.Fraction) when evaluating boundary conditions.

  3. Treating Grid Failure as a Counterexample. A uniform discrete grid samples the interval \([0, 1)\) at finite resolution. If a grid search fails to locate a point where all distances are at least \(1/(k+1)\), this does not mean no such time exists. The valid time intervals can be narrow, slipping between grid points unless the grid denominator is proven to be mathematically sufficient.

  4. Assuming Coprime Reduction Is Always Preserved Under Shifts. While one can divide out \(\gcd(v_1, \dots, v_k)\) to obtain a coprime set of relative speeds, shifting the reference runner from runner \(0\) to runner \(j\) changes the relative speeds to \(|v_i - v_j|\). The greatest common divisor of this new set may differ, and properties that hold for one reference frame must be verified across all frames.

Exercises

  1. Analytical Proof for Two Runners (\(k=1\)). Let runner \(0\) have speed \(0\) and runner \(1\) have speed \(v_1 \in \mathbb{Z}^+\). Prove that \(t = 1/(2v_1)\) is the first time runner \(0\) is lonely. Then show that with \(k = 1\), loneliness only ever happens at isolated instants, not over intervals.

  2. Coprime Invariance. Prove that if the lonely runner conjecture holds for any speed set \(v_1, \dots, v_k\) with \(\gcd(v_1, \dots, v_k) = 1\), then it holds for all speed sets with arbitrary \(\gcd\).

  3. Interval Union Solver. Instead of testing discrete grid points, write a Python program that constructs the forbidden intervals \(I_{i, m} = \left(\frac{m - 1/(k+1)}{v_i}, \frac{m + 1/(k+1)}{v_i}\right)\) on \([0, 1)\) as Fraction intervals. Merge overlapping intervals and verify that their union does not cover \([0, 1)\) for the speed tuple \((1, 3, 4, 7)\).

  4. Symmetry of Witnesses. Show that if \(t \in (0, 1)\) is a witness time satisfying \(\|t v_i\| \ge 1/(k+1)\) for all \(i \in \{1, \dots, k\}\), then \(1 - t\) is also a valid witness time.

Key Takeaways

  • The Lonely Runner Conjecture states that among \(k+1\) runners with distinct constant speeds starting at the same point on a unit circle, each runner will eventually be separated from all other runners by a distance of at least \(1/(k+1)\).
  • In relative coordinates with one runner fixed at the origin, the condition becomes finding \(t > 0\) such that \(\|t v_i\| \ge 1/(k+1)\) for all \(k\) non-zero integer speeds.
  • The isolation bound \(1/(k+1)\) is tight; the consecutive speed sequence \((1, 2, \dots, k)\) cannot achieve any separation strictly greater than \(1/(k+1)\).
  • The conjecture is equivalent to Cusick’s view-obstruction problem: every ray from the origin with positive direction must hit the lattice of cubes \([\frac{1}{k+1}, \frac{k}{k+1}]^k + \mathbb{Z}^k\).
  • Humans proved it up to 7 runners (Barajas and Serra, 2008). Computer-assisted proofs built on Tao’s reduction added 8 runners (Rosenfeld, 2025) and 9 and 10 runners (Trakulthongchai; Rosenfeld independently for 9). The general case and 11 runners are open.
  • The separation function is continuous and piecewise linear, so its maximum is attained at one of finitely many rational critical points. Exact Fraction arithmetic certifies witnesses without floating-point artifacts.
