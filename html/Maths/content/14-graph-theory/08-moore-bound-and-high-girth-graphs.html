Girth, the Moore Bound and High-Girth Regular Graphs

After reading this chapter, you will be able to calculate the girth of any graph, derive and evaluate the Moore bound for arbitrary regular graphs, verify whether candidate graphs attain theoretical optimality, understand why standard random regular graphs fail to produce large cycles, and navigate the algebraic and combinatorial constructions used to build sparse graphs with provably large girth.

The Girth of a Graph

The girth \(g = g(G)\) of a graph \(G\) is the length of its shortest simple cycle. If a graph contains no cycles—that is, if it is a forest or tree—its girth is defined to be infinite (\(g = \infty\)). For graphs that do contain cycles, girth measures the smallest loop through which information, paths, or errors can circulate back to their origin.

In bipartite graphs, every cycle must have even length because every step alternates between independent vertex sets. Consequently, a bipartite graph containing cycles has an even girth: \(g \ge 4\). If a bipartite graph also avoids quadrilaterals (4-cycles), its girth is at least 6.

Girth is a critical design metric across systems engineering:

  • Low-density parity-check (LDPC) codes: LDPC decoding relies on belief propagation passing messages across the edges of a bipartite graph between variable nodes and check nodes. If the code graph contains 4-cycles or 6-cycles, messages rapidly circulate back to their source, creating correlated beliefs and premature decoder convergence. Maximizing girth delays these feedback loops.
  • Network routing and broadcast: In distributed topologies, short cycles create localized broadcast storms and packet looping during route failures. A high-girth network guarantees that alternate paths between neighbors must traverse a long detouring route, ensuring structural diversity.
  • Distributed consensus and local algorithms: In the LOCAL model of distributed computing, nodes exchange state within an \(r\)-hop radius. When an algorithm runs for \(r < g/2\) rounds, the \(r\)-hop neighborhood of every node is guaranteed to be a cycle-free tree, allowing exact tree algorithms to run without cycle interference.

A natural engineering question arises: how sparse can a graph be while maintaining high girth, or conversely, how many vertices must a \(d\)-regular graph possess to achieve a specified girth \(g\)? The answer is governed by the Moore bound.

The Moore Bound: Counting Trees inside Graphs

Consider a simple, undirected graph where every vertex has degree \(d\). When \(d = 2\), the graph is a collection of disjoint cycles; a single cycle \(C_n\) has degree 2, girth \(n\), and exactly \(n\) vertices. For \(d \ge 3\), the graph expands exponentially from any starting point.

Because the graph has girth \(g\), any cycle has length at least \(g\). If we perform a breadth-first search (BFS) up to depth \(\lfloor (g - 1) / 2 \rfloor\), the traversed subgraph cannot contain any cycles. Therefore, the local neighborhood around any vertex or edge must be an exact tree. Counting the minimum number of vertices required to sustain this tree without creating a cycle yields the Moore bound, denoted \(n_0(d, g)\).

The counting splits into two cases depending on whether \(g\) is odd or even.

Odd Girth: \(g = 2t + 1\)

When the girth is odd, say \(g = 2t + 1\), root a BFS tree at an arbitrary vertex \(v\):

  1. Level 0 contains the root \(v\) (\(1\) vertex).
  2. Level 1 contains the \(d\) neighbors of \(v\) (\(d\) vertices).
  3. Each vertex at level \(k - 1 \ge 1\) has \(d - 1\) neighbors other than its parent. Count those as its children at level \(k\). That gives \(d(d - 1)^{k - 1}\) children at level \(k\), for \(k = 1, \dots, t\).

Are all of these vertices distinct? Suppose the same vertex were reached by two different tree paths from \(v\), each of length at most \(t\). The two paths would split at some vertex and meet again, closing a cycle of length at most \(t + t = 2t\). That is impossible when the girth is \(2t + 1\). (Edges between two level-\(t\) vertices are allowed. They close cycles of length \(2t + 1\), and the counting never needs to rule them out.)

The vertices across levels \(0, 1, \dots, t\) are therefore distinct. Summing the vertices across these levels gives the lower bound \(n_0(d, 2t + 1)\):

\[ n_0(d, 2t + 1) = 1 + d \sum_{i=0}^{t-1} (d - 1)^i. \]

For \(d = 2\), this sum is \(1 + 2t = g\). For \(d \ge 3\), applying the geometric series sum \(\sum_{i=0}^{t-1} (d - 1)^i = \frac{(d - 1)^t - 1}{d - 2}\) gives:

\[ n_0(d, 2t + 1) = 1 + d \, \frac{(d - 1)^t - 1}{d - 2}. \]

Even Girth: \(g = 2t\)

When the girth is even, say \(g = 2t\), the same vertex-rooted tree only reaches depth \(t - 1\) safely, which gives a weaker count. Rooting at an edge \(e = \{u, v\}\) gives the tight count:

  1. Distance 0 from the edge: the 2 endpoints \(u, v\).
  2. Distance 1: each endpoint has \(d - 1\) neighbors besides the other endpoint, \(2(d - 1)\) vertices in total.
  3. Distance \(i \le t - 1\): \(2(d - 1)^i\) vertices.

All of these are distinct. If one vertex were reached by two different paths from the edge, each of length at most \(t - 1\), the two paths would close a cycle of length at most \(2(t - 1) + 1 = 2t - 1\) (through the edge \(uv\)) or \(2(t - 1)\) (if both start at the same endpoint). Both are shorter than \(g = 2t\). Summing over distances \(i = 0, 1, \dots, t - 1\) yields:

\[ n_0(d, 2t) = 2 \sum_{i=0}^{t-1} (d - 1)^i. \]

For \(d = 2\), this evaluates to \(2t = g\). For \(d \ge 3\), the geometric sum gives:

\[ n_0(d, 2t) = 2 \, \frac{(d - 1)^t - 1}{d - 2}. \]

The Logarithmic Upper Bound on Girth

Because any \(d\)-regular graph of order \(n\) and girth \(g\) must have at least \(n_0(d, g)\) vertices, we can invert the inequality to establish an upper bound on girth in terms of \(n\):

\[ n \ge n_0(d, g) \ge \frac{2}{d - 2} \left( (d - 1)^{\lfloor g/2 \rfloor} - 1 \right). \]

Taking the base-\((d - 1)\) logarithm of both sides shows that for fixed degree \(d \ge 3\):

\[ g \le 2 \log_{d-1} n + O(1). \]

This logarithmic ceiling is fundamental: no regular topology on \(n\) vertices can ever surpass a girth of roughly \(2 \log_{d-1} n\).

Moore Graphs and the Hoffman-Singleton Theorem

A graph that meets the Moore bound with exact equality is called a Moore graph. A Moore graph of odd girth \(g = 2t + 1\) has diameter \(t\), and any two vertices are joined by a unique path of length at most \(t\).

Moore graphs for odd girth are extraordinarily rare:

  • Girth \(g = 3\) (\(t = 1\)): The complete graph \(K_{d+1}\) has \(n_0(d, 3) = d + 1\) vertices and attains the bound for every \(d\). This is why \(K_4\) shows up as a Moore graph in the script below.
  • Degree \(d = 2\): The odd cycles \(C_{2t + 1}\) have girth \(2t + 1\), order \(2t + 1\), and trivially attain the bound for all \(t \ge 1\).
  • Degree \(d = 3\), Girth \(g = 5\) (\(t = 2\)): The Moore bound requires \(n_0(3, 5) = 1 + 3(1 + 2) = 10\) vertices. This is uniquely realized by the Petersen graph.
  • Degree \(d = 7\), Girth \(g = 5\) (\(t = 2\)): The Moore bound requires \(n_0(7, 5) = 1 + 7(1 + 6) = 50\) vertices. This is uniquely realized by the Hoffman-Singleton graph.

The classification of Moore graphs of diameter 2 (\(g = 5\)) was settled by Alan Hoffman and Robert Singleton in 1960.

The Hoffman-Singleton Theorem (1960)

Let \(G\) be a Moore graph of diameter 2 and degree \(d\). If \(G\) exists, its adjacency matrix \(A\) satisfies:

\[ A^2 + A - (d - 1)I = J, \]

where \(I\) is the identity matrix and \(J\) is the all-ones matrix. The matrix \(J\) has an eigenvalue \(n = d^2 + 1\) with multiplicity 1 (associated with the all-ones eigenvector), and all other eigenvalues of \(J\) are 0.

For any eigenvector orthogonal to the all-ones vector, its eigenvalue \(\lambda\) satisfies the quadratic equation:

\[ \lambda^2 + \lambda - (d - 1) = 0 \implies \lambda = \frac{-1 \pm \sqrt{4d - 3}}{2}. \]

Let these two roots be \(\lambda_1\) and \(\lambda_2\), with multiplicities \(m_1\) and \(m_2\). Because the trace of the adjacency matrix \(A\) is zero (a simple graph has no self-loops):

\[ \operatorname{tr}(A) = d + m_1 \lambda_1 + m_2 \lambda_2 = 0, \qquad m_1 + m_2 = n - 1 = d^2. \]

Substituting \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2\) into the trace equation forces:

\[ 2d - d^2 + (m_1 - m_2)\sqrt{4d - 3} = 0. \]

If \(4d - 3\) is not a perfect square, we must have \(m_1 = m_2\), which forces \(2d - d^2 = 0 \implies d = 2\) (the 5-cycle \(C_5\)). If \(4d - 3\) is a perfect square, let \(s = \sqrt{4d - 3}\), so \(d = (s^2 + 3) / 4\). Substituting \(s\) into the trace expression shows that \(s\) must divide 15, meaning \(s \in \{1, 3, 5, 15\}\). The case \(s = 1\) gives \(d = 1\) (a single edge, which has diameter 1). The remaining values determine the allowable degrees:

  • \(s = 3 \implies d = 3\), \(n = 10\): the Petersen graph.
  • \(s = 5 \implies d = 7\), \(n = 50\): the Hoffman-Singleton graph.
  • \(s = 15 \implies d = 57\), \(n = 3250\): a 57-regular graph on 3,250 vertices.

Whether a 57-regular Moore graph of girth 5 exists is one of the most famous open problems in algebraic graph theory. It cannot be vertex-transitive, and no computer search or algebraic construction has yet confirmed or ruled out its existence.

For diameter \(k \ge 3\) (\(g \ge 7\)), Damerell (1973) and Bannai and Ito (1973) proved that no Moore graphs exist for \(d \ge 3\). Aside from complete graphs \(K_{d+1}\), odd cycles \(C_{2t+1}\), the Petersen graph, and the Hoffman-Singleton graph (plus possibly \(d = 57\)), no Moore graphs exist for odd girth.

Cages and the Gap Beyond the Moore Bound

Because Moore graphs exist for only a handful of parameters, the Moore bound is generally not attainable. This motivates the study of cages.

A \((d, g)\)-cage is a \(d\)-regular graph of girth \(g\) with the minimum possible number of vertices. The order of a \((d, g)\)-cage is denoted \(c(d, g)\). By definition:

\[ c(d, g) \ge n_0(d, g). \]

When \(c(d, g) = n_0(d, g)\), the cage is a Moore graph (for odd girth) or an incidence graph of a generalized polygon (for even girth). When \(c(d, g) > n_0(d, g)\), an excess of vertices is strictly necessary to close the graph into a \(d\)-regular structure without inadvertently creating smaller cycles.

Key examples for 3-regular (cubic) cages illustrate this behavior:

  • \((3, 3)\)-cage: The complete graph \(K_4\) on 4 vertices (\(n_0(3, 3) = 4\)).
  • \((3, 4)\)-cage: The complete bipartite graph \(K_{3, 3}\) on 6 vertices (\(n_0(3, 4) = 6\)).
  • \((3, 5)\)-cage: The Petersen graph on 10 vertices (\(n_0(3, 5) = 10\)).
  • \((3, 6)\)-cage: The Heawood graph on 14 vertices (\(n_0(3, 6) = 14\)). It is the incidence graph of the Fano plane (the projective plane \(PG(2, 2)\)).
  • \((3, 7)\)-cage: The McGee graph on 24 vertices. Here, the Moore bound is \(n_0(3, 7) = 1 + 3(1 + 2 + 4) = 22\). It has been proved that no 3-regular graph of girth 7 can exist on 22 vertices; 24 vertices are required.
  • \((3, 8)\)-cage: The Tutte-Coxeter graph (also known as the Tutte 8-cage) on 30 vertices (\(n_0(3, 8) = 2(1 + 2 + 4 + 8) = 30\)). It is the incidence graph of the generalized quadrangle \(W(2)\).

The following table summarizes small cubic cages and their relationship to the Moore bound:

\((3, 4)\) Utility \(K_{3, 3}\) 6 6 Yes

Measuring Girth and Checking the Bound in Code

To compute the girth of an arbitrary unweighted graph, we can perform a BFS starting from each vertex \(u\). During the BFS:

  1. Maintain dist[x] (distance from the root) and parent[x] (predecessor in the BFS tree).
  2. If an edge \((x, y)\) is encountered where \(y\) has already been visited and \(y \ne \text{parent}[x]\), a cycle is discovered. Its length is \(\text{dist}[x] + \text{dist}[y] + 1\).
  3. If \(2 \cdot \text{dist}[x] + 1\) matches or exceeds the shortest cycle found so far, the BFS branch can be pruned early.
  4. If a 3-cycle (triangle) is detected, the algorithm can terminate immediately, as no simple graph can have a girth smaller than 3.

The following script implements this algorithm and tests five canonical graph structures: the Petersen graph (built as the Kneser graph \(K(5, 2)\)), the Heawood graph (built from Fano plane point-line incidences), a cycle \(C_7\), the complete graph \(K_4\), and the 3-dimensional hypercube \(Q_3\).

"""BFS-based girth computation and Moore bound verification."""

from collections import deque
from itertools import combinations


def moore_bound(d: int, g: int) -> int:
    """Compute the Moore lower bound n0(d, g) on the number of vertices."""
    if d <= 1:
        return 2
    if d == 2:
        return g
    t = g // 2
    if g % 2 == 1:
        return 1 + d * sum((d - 1) ** i for i in range(t))
    else:
        return 2 * sum((d - 1) ** i for i in range(t))


def compute_girth(adj: dict[int, list[int]]) -> int:
    """Find the girth of a graph using breadth-first search from each vertex."""
    best = float("inf")
    for root in adj:
        dist = {root: 0}
        parent = {root: None}
        q = deque([root])
        while q:
            u = q.popleft()
            if dist[u] * 2 + 1 >= best:
                break
            for v in adj[u]:
                if v not in dist:
                    dist[v] = dist[u] + 1
                    parent[v] = u
                    q.append(v)
                elif parent[u] != v:
                    best = min(best, dist[u] + dist[v] + 1)
                    if best == 3:
                        return 3
    return int(best) if best != float("inf") else 0


def build_petersen() -> dict[int, list[int]]:
    """Petersen graph as Kneser graph K(5,2): 2-subsets of 5 elements, adjacent when disjoint."""
    subsets = list(combinations(range(5), 2))
    n = len(subsets)
    adj: dict[int, list[int]] = {i: [] for i in range(n)}
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i + 1, n):
            if not (set(subsets[i]) & set(subsets[j])):
                adj[i].append(j)
                adj[j].append(i)
    return adj


def build_heawood() -> dict[int, list[int]]:
    """Heawood graph as the bipartite incidence graph of the Fano plane PG(2,2)."""
    # 7 points (0..6) and 7 lines (7..13)
    # Line i contains points {i, i+1, i+3} mod 7
    adj: dict[int, list[int]] = {i: [] for i in range(14)}
    for line_idx in range(7):
        pts = [(line_idx + offset) % 7 for offset in (0, 1, 3)]
        line_vert = 7 + line_idx
        for pt in pts:
            adj[pt].append(line_vert)
            adj[line_vert].append(pt)
    return adj


def build_cycle(n: int) -> dict[int, list[int]]:
    """Cycle graph C_n."""
    adj: dict[int, list[int]] = {i: [] for i in range(n)}
    for i in range(n):
        nxt = (i + 1) % n
        adj[i].append(nxt)
        adj[nxt].append(i)
    return adj


def build_complete(n: int) -> dict[int, list[int]]:
    """Complete graph K_n."""
    adj: dict[int, list[int]] = {i: [] for i in range(n)}
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i + 1, n):
            adj[i].append(j)
            adj[j].append(i)
    return adj


def build_hypercube(dim: int) -> dict[int, list[int]]:
    """dim-dimensional hypercube Q_dim."""
    n = 1 << dim
    adj: dict[int, list[int]] = {i: [] for i in range(n)}
    for i in range(n):
        for bit in range(dim):
            nbr = i ^ (1 << bit)
            if nbr > i:
                adj[i].append(nbr)
                adj[nbr].append(i)
    return adj


def main() -> None:
    graphs = [
        ("Petersen K(5,2)", build_petersen()),
        ("Heawood", build_heawood()),
        ("Cycle C7", build_cycle(7)),
        ("Complete K4", build_complete(4)),
        ("Cube Q3", build_hypercube(3)),
    ]

    header = f"{'Graph':<18} {'n':>5} {'deg':>5} {'girth':>7} {'Moore n0':>10} {'Attains bound':>15}"
    print(header)
    print("-" * len(header))
    for name, adj in graphs:
        n = len(adj)
        deg = len(adj[0])
        g = compute_girth(adj)
        n0 = moore_bound(deg, g)
        attains = "Yes" if n == n0 else "No"
        print(f"{name:<18} {n:>5} {deg:>5} {g:>7} {n0:>10} {attains:>15}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

  • The Petersen graph (\(n = 10, d = 3, g = 5\)), Heawood graph (\(n = 14, d = 3, g = 6\)), 7-cycle (\(n = 7, d = 2, g = 7\)), and complete graph \(K_4\) (\(n = 4, d = 3, g = 3\)) all have vertex counts matching their respective Moore bounds (\(n = n_0\)).
  • The 3-cube \(Q_3\) (\(n = 8, d = 3, g = 4\)) has a Moore bound of \(n_0(3, 4) = 6\). Its actual vertex count of 8 exceeds the Moore bound. The true \((3, 4)\)-cage is \(K_{3, 3}\) on 6 vertices.

Why Random Regular Graphs Fail to Achieve High Girth

In many areas of graph algorithms, sampling a random regular graph produces ideal properties: random \(d\)-regular graphs are almost always expanders, possess nearly optimal spectral gaps, and have logarithmic diameter. It is tempting to assume that random regular graphs also have high girth.

This intuition is entirely false. In a random regular graph, the number of short cycles does not vanish as \(n\) grows.

To sample a random \(d\)-regular graph on \(n\) vertices, we use the configuration model (Bollobás 1980):

  1. Replace each vertex \(v \in \{0, \dots, n - 1\}\) with \(d\) distinct half-edges (or “points”), yielding \(n \cdot d\) points in total.
  2. Form a uniform random matching of the \(n \cdot d\) points into \((n \cdot d) / 2\) pairs.
  3. Collapse the paired points back to their corresponding vertices.
  4. If the resulting multigraph contains self-loops or multi-edges, reject it and repeat.

For fixed degree \(d \ge 3\), the probability that the configuration model yields a simple graph approaches \(\exp(-(d^2 - 1) / 4)\) as \(n \to \infty\). For \(d = 3\), this acceptance rate is \(\exp(-2) \approx 13.53\%\), making rejection sampling straightforward.

The distribution of short cycles in random regular graphs is governed by the Poisson distribution. Let \(X_k\) denote the number of cycles of length \(k\). As \(n \to \infty\), the random variables \(X_3, X_4, \dots, X_k\) converge to independent Poisson variables with parameters:

\[ \lambda_k = \lim_{n \to \infty} \mathbb{E}[X_k] = \frac{(d - 1)^k}{2k}. \]

Notice that \(\lambda_k\) depends exclusively on the degree \(d\) and cycle length \(k\)—it is completely independent of \(n\).

For cubic graphs (\(d = 3\)):

  • For triangles (\(k = 3\)): \(\lambda_3 = 2^3 / (2 \cdot 3) = 8 / 6 \approx 1.333\). The probability that a random 3-regular graph has no triangles is \(\exp(-4/3) \approx 0.264\).
  • For quadrilaterals (\(k = 4\)): \(\lambda_4 = 2^4 / (2 \cdot 4) = 16 / 8 = 2.0\). The probability of avoiding 4-cycles is \(\exp(-2) \approx 0.135\).
  • For pentagons (\(k = 5\)): \(\lambda_5 = 2^5 / (2 \cdot 5) = 32 / 10 = 3.2\).

The probability that a random 3-regular graph has girth greater than 5 is:

\[ \Pr[X_3 = 0, X_4 = 0, X_5 = 0] \approx \exp(-1.333 - 2.0 - 3.2) = \exp(-6.533) \approx 0.00145. \]

Even if \(n = 10^9\), a random 3-regular graph contains a triangle, a 4-cycle or a 5-cycle with probability about \(0.998\). The girth of a random regular graph stays bounded in probability as \(n \to \infty\). It does not grow like \(\log n\).

The script below demonstrates this phenomenon empirically using seeded rejection sampling across sizes \(n \in \{16, 64, 256, 1024\}\).

"""Empirical girth distribution of random 3-regular graphs via the configuration model."""

import math
import random
import statistics
from collections import deque


def compute_girth(adj: list[list[int]]) -> int:
    """Compute girth via BFS from each vertex, stopping early when 3 is found."""
    n = len(adj)
    best = float("inf")
    for start in range(n):
        dist = {start: 0}
        parent = {start: None}
        q = deque([start])
        while q:
            u = q.popleft()
            if dist[u] * 2 + 1 >= best:
                break
            for v in adj[u]:
                if v not in dist:
                    dist[v] = dist[u] + 1
                    parent[v] = u
                    q.append(v)
                elif parent[u] != v:
                    best = min(best, dist[u] + dist[v] + 1)
                    if best == 3:
                        return 3
    return int(best) if best != float("inf") else 0


def generate_random_3_regular(n: int, rng: random.Random, max_attempts: int = 1000) -> list[list[int]]:
    """Generate a random 3-regular graph on n vertices using the configuration model with rejection."""
    points = [v for v in range(n) for _ in range(3)]
    m = len(points)
    for _ in range(max_attempts):
        rng.shuffle(points)
        adj: list[list[int]] = [[] for _ in range(n)]
        valid = True
        for i in range(0, m, 2):
            u, v = points[i], points[i + 1]
            if u == v or v in adj[u]:
                valid = False
                break
            adj[u].append(v)
            adj[v].append(u)
        if valid:
            return adj
    raise RuntimeError(f"Failed to generate simple 3-regular graph on n={n} after {max_attempts} attempts.")


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(42)
    sizes = [16, 64, 256, 1024]
    num_samples = 20

    header = f"{'n':>5} {'min':>5} {'median':>7} {'max':>5} {'log2(n)':>10} {'2*log2(n)':>10}"
    print(header)
    print("-" * len(header))
    for n in sizes:
        girths = []
        for _ in range(num_samples):
            graph = generate_random_3_regular(n, rng)
            g = compute_girth(graph)
            girths.append(g)
        med = statistics.median(girths)
        log2_val = math.log2(n)
        two_log2_val = 2.0 * log2_val
        print(f"{n:>5} {min(girths):>5} {med:>7.1f} {max(girths):>5} {log2_val:>10.2f} {two_log2_val:>10.2f}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

Achieving girth that scales with \(\log n\) requires structured, non-random engineering.

High-Girth Constructions: From Probabilistic Proofs to Ramanujan Graphs

If naive random graphs fail, how can we construct regular graphs whose girth scales with \(\log_{d-1} n\)? The development of high-girth graphs unfolded in three stages: non-constructive existence proofs, explicit algebraic constructions, and modern breakthroughs.

1. The Erdős-Sachs Theorem (1963)

In 1959, Paul Erdős introduced the probabilistic method to graph theory by proving that graphs with simultaneously arbitrarily high girth and arbitrarily high chromatic number exist. Erdős sampled an Erdős-Rényi random graph \(G(n, p)\), showed that the number of short cycles was small compared to \(n\), and deleted one vertex from each short cycle. While this established existence, the resulting graph was irregular and non-constructive.

In 1963, Paul Erdős and Horst Sachs resolved the regular case. They proved by a counting argument that for every \(d \ge 3\) and \(g \ge 3\) there is a \(d\)-regular graph of girth at least \(g\) with roughly \((d - 1)^{g}\) vertices, up to a constant factor. Inverting this gives infinite families with

\[ g \ge (1 - o(1)) \log_{d-1} n. \]

The argument proves that such graphs exist but gives no efficient way to construct them.

2. Lubotzky-Phillips-Sarnak (LPS) Ramanujan Graphs (1988)

Engineers designing LDPC codes, interconnect fabrics, or cryptographic hash functions cannot work with non-constructive proofs; they require explicit adjacency rules.

Explicit constructions came first from Margulis (1982), improved by Imrich to girth about \(\frac{4}{9} \log_{d-1} n\). The breakthrough came in 1988, when Alexander Lubotzky, Ralph Phillips and Peter Sarnak, and independently Gregory Margulis, constructed Ramanujan graphs with larger girth. These graphs are explicit Cayley graphs constructed over projective linear groups:

  • Fix distinct primes \(p, q \equiv 1 \pmod 4\). The degree is \(d = p + 1\).
  • If \(p\) is a quadratic residue mod \(q\), the vertices are the elements of \(PSL_2(\mathbb{F}_q)\). Otherwise they are the elements of \(PGL_2(\mathbb{F}_q)\), and the graph is bipartite.
  • Generators correspond to the \(p + 1\) integer solutions to \(a_0^2 + a_1^2 + a_2^2 + a_3^2 = p\) with \(a_0 > 0\) odd and \(a_1, a_2, a_3\) even, mapped into \(PGL_2(\mathbb{F}_q)\) via Hamilton quaternions.

The LPS graphs achieved two milestones simultaneously:

  1. Optimal expansion (Ramanujan bound): Every non-trivial eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix satisfies \(|\lambda| \le 2\sqrt{d - 1}\).
  2. High girth: By analyzing the representation of integers by quaternary quadratic forms, LPS proved that the girth of these Cayley graphs satisfies, asymptotically (the bipartite family attains the constant \(4/3\); the non-bipartite family has girth at least \(\frac{2}{3}\log_{d-1} n\)): \[ g \ge \frac{4}{3} \log_{d-1} n - c. \]

The factor \(4/3 \approx 1.333\) beat even the non-constructive Erdős–Sachs constant of 1. It remained the best known constant for explicit families (Weiss matched it for cubic graphs) from 1988 until 2026.

3. The 2026 Frontier: Surpassing the 4/3 Barrier

Recall the fundamental limits:

  • Moore upper bound: \(g \le 2.000 \log_{d-1} n + O(1)\).
  • Erdős-Sachs existence (non-constructive): \(g \ge 1.000 \log_{d-1} n - O(1)\).
  • LPS Ramanujan graphs (explicit): \(g \ge \frac{4}{3} \log_{d-1} n - O(1) \approx 1.333 \log_{d-1} n\).

The gap between \(4/3\) and 2 remained open for decades. In October 2026, doubleAI released a paper (PDF) that the company says was written by its AI agent. For every prime power \(q\), it constructs an explicit infinite family of \((q^3 + 1)\)-regular graphs with

\[ g \ge \frac{8}{5} \log_{q^3} n - O(1) = 1.6 \log_{d-1} n - O(1), \qquad d - 1 = q^3. \]

The graphs are quotients of a Bruhat–Tits tree by congruence subgroups of an arithmetic group built from a division algebra of degree four. The authors report that the main theorem is formalized in Lean on Mathlib. For one family of 28-regular graphs (\(q = 3\)), they also prove a matching upper bound: the girth there is exactly \(\frac{8}{5} \log_{27} n + O(1)\).

Two caveats. The improvement covers only degrees of the form \(q^3 + 1\) (9, 28, 65, 126, …), not every degree. And as of November 2026 it is a company-released preprint, not a peer-reviewed publication. Treat the \(8/5\) constant as a strong new claim, backed by a machine-checked proof, that is still being absorbed by the field.

Common Mistakes

  1. Confusing odd and even Moore formulas: When evaluating candidate graphs, remember that odd girth \(g = 2t + 1\) roots a tree at a single vertex (\(1 + d \sum (d-1)^i\)), while even girth \(g = 2t\) roots a tree across an entire edge (\(2 \sum (d-1)^i\)). Applying the odd formula to an even-girth graph overestimates the required vertex count.
  2. Assuming good expanders have high girth: Random regular graphs have excellent spectral expansion (\(\lambda_2 \approx 2\sqrt{d - 1}\)) and logarithmic diameter, but their girth does not grow with \(n\). For \(d = 3\) it is 3 about 74% of the time. Expansion measures path connectivity and cut size; girth measures the absence of short local cycles.
  3. Assuming cages always achieve the Moore bound: Aside from Moore graphs and incidence graphs of generalized polygons, \((d, g)\)-cages strictly exceed \(n_0(d, g)\). For example, the \((3, 7)\)-cage (McGee graph) requires 24 vertices, whereas \(n_0(3, 7) = 22\).
  4. Conflating diameter and girth bounds: The Moore bound for diameter \(k\) states \(n \le 1 + d \sum_{i=0}^{k-1}(d-1)^i\). The Moore bound for girth \(g\) states \(n \ge n_0(d, g)\). A graph can attain the girth bound without attaining the diameter bound (for instance, bipartite even-girth cages have diameter \(g/2\)).
  5. Relying on random edge-swapping to eliminate short cycles: A common heuristic in code design is to generate a random regular graph and perform 2-switch edge swaps to break triangles. While this can eliminate 3-cycles, each swap creates new paths that frequently introduce 4-cycles or 5-cycles. Increasing the girth beyond 6 or 7 by local edge perturbation becomes computationally intractable as \(n\) grows.

Key takeaways

  • The girth \(g\) of a graph is the length of its shortest cycle. Forests have \(g = \infty\), while bipartite graphs have even girth.
  • The Moore bound \(n_0(d, g)\) establishes the minimum number of vertices in a \(d\)-regular graph of girth \(g\):
    • Odd girth \(g = 2t + 1\): \(n_0(d, 2t + 1) = 1 + d \sum_{i=0}^{t-1} (d - 1)^i\).
    • Even girth \(g = 2t\): \(n_0(d, 2t) = 2 \sum_{i=0}^{t-1} (d - 1)^i\).
  • Inverting the Moore bound establishes the universal logarithmic ceiling \(g \le 2 \log_{d-1} n + O(1)\).
  • Graphs achieving the Moore bound are Moore graphs. The Hoffman-Singleton theorem proves that diameter-2 Moore graphs (\(g = 5\)) can exist only for degree \(d \in \{2, 3, 7, 57\}\). The degree 57 case remains unsolved.
  • When \(n_0(d, g)\) cannot be attained, the minimal graph is called a \((d, g)\)-cage. The Heawood graph is the \((3, 6)\)-cage (\(n = 14\)), the McGee graph is the \((3, 7)\)-cage (\(n = 24\), exceeding \(n_0 = 22\)), and the Tutte-Coxeter graph is the \((3, 8)\)-cage (\(n = 30\)).
  • In random \(d\)-regular graphs, cycle counts \(X_k\) converge to independent Poisson variables whose means \(\lambda_k = (d - 1)^k / (2k)\) do not depend on \(n\). As a result, the girth stays bounded in probability.
  • Explicit constructions with logarithmic girth require algebraic structure: the LPS Ramanujan graphs (1988) achieved \(g \ge \frac{4}{3} \log_{d-1} n\), and an October 2026 preprint from doubleAI claims \(g \ge \frac{8}{5} \log_{d-1} n\) for degrees \(q^3 + 1\).
