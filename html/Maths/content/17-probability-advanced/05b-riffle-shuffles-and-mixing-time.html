How Many Riffle Shuffles Mix a Deck? Gilbert-Shannon-Reeds and Total Variation

After reading this chapter, you will be able to implement the Gilbert–Shannon–Reeds riffle shuffle model in code, evaluate permutation likelihoods using the Bayer–Diaconis formula, compute rising sequences and Eulerian numbers, calculate exact total variation distances against the uniform distribution, and explain why card decks undergo an abrupt mixing cutoff rather than gradual exponential decay.

Riffle shuffling—splitting a deck into two halves and interleaving them with your thumbs—is the standard way card players randomize a deck. Edgar Gilbert and Claude Shannon (in a 1955 Bell Labs memorandum), and independently Jim Reeds (in unpublished 1981 notes), formalized a physical model of this motion. In 1992, Dave Bayer and Persi Diaconis analyzed the model exactly. Their analysis is behind the slogan “seven shuffles suffice” for a 52-card deck. The precise meaning of that slogan, a total variation distance that first drops below \(1/2\) at seven shuffles, is what this chapter computes.

All listings are complete Python files written for Python 3.14 using only the standard library (random, math, fractions, itertools, and collections). Monte Carlo simulations use fixed seeds for reproducibility.

1. The Gilbert–Shannon–Reeds (GSR) Model

When an experienced dealer riffles a deck of \(n\) cards, the process breaks into two distinct mechanical phases:

  1. The Cut. The dealer splits the deck of \(n\) cards into two packets: a left packet of size \(c\) and a right packet of size \(n - c\). The cut is not exact; it fluctuates around \(n/2\) according to a binomial distribution: \[P(\text{cut} = c) = \frac{1}{2^n} \binom{n}{c}.\] This matches the physical reality of picking up cards one by one into the left hand by independent fair coin flips.

  2. The Interleaving (Riffle). The dealer releases cards from the thumbs. If the left packet currently holds \(a\) cards and the right packet holds \(b\) cards, the probability that the next card drops from the left packet is proportional to its remaining thickness: \[P(\text{drop from left} \mid a, b) = \frac{a}{a + b}, \quad P(\text{drop from right} \mid a, b) = \frac{b}{a + b}.\] Cards drop until both packets are exhausted.

This process defines a Markov chain on the symmetric group \(S_n\). Because every card in the left packet retains its relative order to the other left cards, and every card in the right packet retains its relative order to the other right cards, an interleaving preserves the internal order of both halves.

A fundamental combinatorial identity connects the cut and the interleaving. Given a cut of size \(c\), take any specific sequence of drops that uses \(c\) left cards and \(n - c\) right cards. At each step the denominator is the number of cards still in hand: \(n, n-1, \dots, 1\). The numerators are the remaining sizes of whichever packet drops. Over the whole sequence these run through \(c, c-1, \dots, 1\) for the left packet and \(n-c, \dots, 1\) for the right, in some interleaved order. So the probability is the same for every sequence: \[\frac{c!\,(n - c)!}{n!} = \frac{1}{\binom{n}{c}}.\]

Every one of the \(\binom{n}{c}\) possible interleavings of the two packets is equally likely. Multiplying the cut probability by the interleaving probability reveals that any valid interleaved arrangement of a cut at \(c\) has unconditional probability: \[P(\text{cut} = c) \cdot \frac{1}{\binom{n}{c}} = \frac{\binom{n}{c}}{2^n} \cdot \frac{1}{\binom{n}{c}} = \frac{1}{2^n}.\]

The probability of producing a permutation depends solely on the number of ways it can be decomposed into two ordered packets. A GSR shuffle is a random walk on \(S_n\) driven by an irreducible, aperiodic transition matrix. Consequently, repeated independent GSR shuffles converge uniquely to the uniform distribution \(U(\pi) = 1/n!\).

2. Rising Sequences and the Inverse Shuffle View

To understand how shuffles accumulate, consider what happens to the card labels. Let the cards start in sorted order labeled \(1, 2, \dots, n\) from top to bottom.

Rising Sequences

A rising sequence of a permutation \(\pi \in S_n\) is a maximal subsequence of consecutive card values that appear in increasing order within the shuffled deck.

Consider a 6-card deck shuffled into the order \((1, 4, 2, 5, 3, 6)\): - Starting with 1, we look for 2 (found later at index 2), then 3 (at index 4). The subsequence \((1, 2, 3)\) is in increasing order of positions. - Starting with 4, we look for 5 (found at index 3), then 6 (found at index 5). The subsequence \((4, 5, 6)\) is also in increasing order of positions.

The deck \((1, 4, 2, 5, 3, 6)\) consists of exactly 2 rising sequences: \(\{1, 2, 3\}\) and \(\{4, 5, 6\}\).

There is an exact duality between rising sequences and descents. Let \(\pi^{-1}(x)\) denote the position of card \(x\) in the shuffled deck. Card \(x+1\) appears before card \(x\) in the deck if and only if: \[\pi^{-1}(x+1) < \pi^{-1}(x).\]

Such an occurrence is a descent in the inverse permutation \(\pi^{-1}\). Each descent breaks a rising sequence and starts a new one. Therefore, if a permutation \(\pi\) has \(r\) rising sequences, its inverse \(\pi^{-1}\) has exactly \(r - 1\) descents: \[r(\pi) = 1 + \sum_{x=1}^{n-1} \mathbf{1}_{\{\pi^{-1}(x+1) < \pi^{-1}(x)\}}.\]

Because a single riffle shuffle cuts the deck into two packets and merges them while preserving each packet’s internal order, a single riffle shuffle can produce permutations with at most 2 rising sequences.

The \(a\)-Shuffle View

Bayer and Diaconis introduced a perspective called an \(a\)-shuffle. Instead of cutting into 2 packets, imagine cutting the deck into \(a\) packets according to a multinomial distribution \((p_1, \dots, p_a) \sim \text{Multinomial}(n; 1/a, \dots, 1/a)\), and dropping cards from the packets with probability proportional to their remaining sizes.

Performing \(k\) sequential riffle shuffles is mathematically identical to a single \(a\)-shuffle with \(a = 2^k\).

The inverse of an \(a\)-shuffle has a remarkably simple description: 1. Assign each card \(x \in \{1, \dots, n\}\) an independent, uniformly distributed label \(L(x) \in \{1, 2, \dots, a\}\). 2. Stable-sort the deck according to the labels \(L(x)\), keeping cards with the same label in their original relative order.

Under this inverse view, card \(x+1\) ends up before card \(x\) in the shuffled deck if and only if \(L(x+1) < L(x)\). If \(L(x+1) \ge L(x)\), their relative order is preserved. Each strict drop in the labels needs a new, smaller label. So a permutation with \(r\) rising sequences needs at least \(r\) distinct labels, which is why an \(a\)-shuffle can only reach permutations with \(r \le a\).

3. The Bayer–Diaconis Distribution Formula

Using the \(a\)-shuffle framework with \(a = 2^k\), Bayer and Diaconis (1992) proved that the probability of observing any permutation \(\pi \in S_n\) after \(k\) shuffles depends strictly on its number of rising sequences \(r = r(\pi)\).

For a deck of \(n\) cards after \(k\) independent GSR shuffles: \[P_k(\pi) = \frac{\binom{2^k + n - r}{n}}{2^{kn}}.\]

If \(2^k < r\), the binomial coefficient vanishes, reflecting the fact that \(k\) shuffles cannot generate a permutation requiring more than \(2^k\) rising sequences.

Parameter Interpretation
\(n\) Total number of cards in the deck
\(k\) Number of independent riffle shuffles performed
\(r = r(\pi)\) Number of rising sequences in permutation \(\pi\) (\(1 \le r \le n\))
\(2^k\) Effective number of packets after \(k\) binary splits
\(P_k(\pi)\) Exact probability of generating permutation \(\pi\)

Notice what this implies: - The identity permutation \(\pi = \text{id}\) has \(r = 1\) rising sequence. After 1 shuffle (\(k=1\)), \(P_1(\text{id}) = \binom{2 + n - 1}{n} / 2^n = (n+1)/2^n\). The identity can be produced by any of the \(n+1\) possible cut points. - Any permutation with \(r = 2\) rising sequences has \(P_1(\pi) = \binom{2 + n - 2}{n} / 2^n = 1/2^n\). - Any permutation with \(r \ge 3\) cannot be reached in a single shuffle: \(P_1(\pi) = 0\).

All permutations with the same number of rising sequences share identical probabilities under \(P_k\).

4. Numerical Verification: Simulating GSR Shuffles

To verify the GSR model and the Bayer–Diaconis formula, we simulate \(k = 1, 2, 3\) shuffles on a 4-card deck. A 4-card deck has \(4! = 24\) permutations. We run 200,000 Monte Carlo trials and compare empirical frequencies against \(P_k(\pi)\).

import itertools
import math
import random
from collections import Counter


def gsr_shuffle(deck: list[int], rng: random.Random) -> list[int]:
    """Execute a single Gilbert-Shannon-Reeds riffle shuffle."""
    n = len(deck)
    # Binomial cut: count successes of n independent fair coin tosses
    cut = sum(rng.random() < 0.5 for _ in range(n))
    left = deck[:cut]
    right = deck[cut:]

    out: list[int] = []
    i, j = 0, 0
    while i < len(left) or j < len(right):
        rem_left = len(left) - i
        rem_right = len(right) - j
        # Drop card with probability proportional to remaining packet size
        if rng.random() < rem_left / (rem_left + rem_right):
            out.append(left[i])
            i += 1
        else:
            out.append(right[j])
            j += 1
    return out


def count_rising_sequences(deck: list[int] | tuple[int, ...]) -> int:
    """Compute rising sequences via descents in the inverse permutation."""
    pos = {card: idx for idx, card in enumerate(deck)}
    descents = sum(1 for c in range(len(deck) - 1) if pos[c + 1] < pos[c])
    return descents + 1


def bayer_diaconis_prob(n: int, r: int, k: int) -> float:
    """Exact probability P_k(pi) from Bayer and Diaconis (1992)."""
    return math.comb(2**k + n - r, n) / (2 ** (k * n))


def main() -> None:
    rng = random.Random(20261017)
    n = 4
    trials = 200_000
    perms = list(itertools.permutations(range(n)))
    r_map = {p: count_rising_sequences(p) for p in perms}

    print(f"Simulating {trials} trials for n = {n} cards across k = 1, 2, 3 shuffles:")
    for k in (1, 2, 3):
        counts: Counter[tuple[int, ...]] = Counter()
        for _ in range(trials):
            deck = list(range(n))
            for _ in range(k):
                deck = gsr_shuffle(deck, rng)
            counts[tuple(deck)] += 1

        max_err = max(
            abs(counts[p] / trials - bayer_diaconis_prob(n, r_map[p], k))
            for p in perms
        )
        print(f"k = {k}: max absolute error = {max_err:.5f}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

5. Total Variation Distance and the Cutoff Phenomenon

To measure how close a shuffled deck distribution \(P_k\) is to the uniform distribution \(U\), probabilists use the total variation distance: \[\|P_k - U\|_{\text{TV}} = \max_{A \subseteq S_n} |P_k(A) - U(A)| = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{\pi \in S_n} \left| P_k(\pi) - \frac{1}{n!} \right|.\]

Total variation distance has a direct operational meaning: if an observer devises the best possible statistical test or betting strategy to distinguish a shuffled deck from a truly randomized deck, their maximum possible advantage is exactly \(\|P_k - U\|_{\text{TV}}\). - When \(\|P_k - U\|_{\text{TV}} \approx 1\), there exists an event \(A\) that almost certainly happens under one distribution and almost never under the other. The deck is not random. - When \(\|P_k - U\|_{\text{TV}} \le 0.5\), the advantage drops significantly. - When \(\|P_k - U\|_{\text{TV}} \to 0\), the distributions become indistinguishable.

The Cutoff Phenomenon

Many Markov chains approach stationarity via a gradual geometric decay: \(\|P_k - U\|_{\text{TV}} \le C \lambda^k\) from the very first step. High-dimensional random walks on groups, however, often exhibit the cutoff phenomenon.

Instead of decaying gradually, total variation distance stays stubbornly close to \(1\) for several steps, and then collapses abruptly to 0 over a narrow window.

For a deck of \(n\) cards shuffled by GSR riffles, Bayer and Diaconis proved that, to leading order as \(n \to \infty\), the mixing time undergoes cutoff at: \[k^* = \frac{3}{2} \log_2 n.\]

For a standard deck of \(n = 52\) cards, \(\frac{3}{2} \log_2(52) \approx 8.55\). This is an asymptotic statement, so for a finite deck it locates the window rather than an exact step. As we will compute explicitly, the curve for 52 cards drops below \(1/2\) at shuffle 7.

6. Exact Mixing Table for 52 Cards

Because \(P_k(\pi)\) depends only on the number of rising sequences \(r\), we do not need to sum over all \(52! \approx 8.0658 \times 10^{67}\) permutations individually.

We group permutations by their number of rising sequences. The number of permutations in \(S_n\) with \(r\) rising sequences equals the Eulerian number \(A(n, r-1)\), which counts permutations with \(r-1\) descents in their inverse: \[\sum_{\pi \in S_n} \mathbf{1}_{\{r(\pi) = r\}} = A(n, r-1).\]

Eulerian numbers satisfy the standard recurrence relation: \[A(n, m) = (n - m) A(n - 1, m - 1) + (m + 1) A(n - 1, m),\] with base cases \(A(1, 0) = 1\) and \(A(n, m) = 0\) for \(m < 0\) or \(m \ge n\).

Using Eulerian numbers, the total variation distance simplifies to a 1D sum of 52 terms: \[\|P_k - U\|_{\text{TV}} = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{r=1}^n A(n, r-1) \cdot \left| \frac{\binom{2^k + n - r}{n}}{2^{kn}} - \frac{1}{n!} \right|.\]

import math
from fractions import Fraction


def compute_eulerian_row(n: int) -> list[int]:
    """Compute row n of Eulerian numbers A(n, m) for m = 0 ... n-1."""
    row = [1]
    for i in range(2, n + 1):
        new_row = [0] * i
        for m in range(i):
            left_term = (i - m) * (row[m - 1] if m - 1 >= 0 else 0)
            right_term = (m + 1) * (row[m] if m < len(row) else 0)
            new_row[m] = left_term + right_term
        row = new_row
    return row


def compute_tv(n: int, k_max: int) -> list[tuple[int, float]]:
    """Compute exact total variation distance for k = 1 ... k_max using Fractions."""
    row = compute_eulerian_row(n)
    n_fact = math.factorial(n)
    results = []

    for k in range(1, k_max + 1):
        two_k = 2**k
        two_kn = 2 ** (k * n)
        tv_sum = Fraction(0, 1)

        for r in range(1, n + 1):
            count = row[r - 1]  # A(n, r - 1)
            p_k = Fraction(math.comb(two_k + n - r, n), two_kn)
            u = Fraction(1, n_fact)
            tv_sum += count * abs(p_k - u)

        tv = tv_sum / 2
        results.append((k, float(tv)))

    return results


def main() -> None:
    n = 52
    k_max = 12
    print(f"Exact Total Variation Distance for n = {n} Cards")
    print("--------------------------------------------------")
    print(f"{'k':>3}   {'TV Distance':>12}")
    print("--------------------------------------------------")
    for k, tv in compute_tv(n, k_max):
        print(f"{k:3d}   {tv:12.3f}")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

This is the origin of the rule of thumb that “seven shuffles mix a deck.” - Beyond \(k = 7\), the distance halves with each subsequent shuffle, decaying geometrically as the uniform distribution takes over.

7. Imperfect Riffles: Sloppy Cuts

The exact formula above depends on two GSR details. Each card drops with probability proportional to its packet’s remaining size, and the cut is binomial, so it lands close to \(n/2\). Real shufflers cut high or low.

  • Lalley (2000) studied asymmetric binomial cuts \(\text{Bin}(n, p)\). He identified cold spots: runs of originally adjacent cards that keep their relative order long after the rest of the deck looks mixed.
  • Sellke (2022) completed the cutoff result for asymmetric binomial cuts for all \(p \in (0, 1)\).
  • Sellke, Shi and Wang (arXiv 2510.22783) prove cutoff for essentially any cut-size distribution. If the cut fractions converge to a law \(\mu\) on \([0, 1]\), mixing happens after \(\overline{C}_\mu \log n\) shuffles, for an explicit constant \(\overline{C}_\mu\). The result also covers cut sizes that vary from shuffle to shuffle, and multi-pile cuts. For uniformly random cuts, \(\overline{C}_\mu \approx 3.606\), which gives about \(14.25\) shuffles for 52 cards. That is roughly double the GSR answer.

Every result here still assumes that cards drop one at a time. Shuffles where cards fall in clumps remain open. Quanta Magazine’s June 2026 article tells the story behind the new proof.

8. Pitfalls and Common Mistakes

  • Confusing card position with card value. A rising sequence tracks consecutive values \((x, x+1, \dots)\) across the deck, which corresponds to descents in the inverse permutation \(\pi^{-1}\), not runs in \(\pi\) itself. Checking whether \(\pi(i) < \pi(i+1)\) calculates descents of \(\pi\), which does not equal the number of rising sequences.
  • Assuming mixing is smooth and gradual. Engineers accustomed to physical diffusion or thermal relaxation often expect total variation to decay exponentially from the start (\(e^{-ck}\)). Assuming five shuffles is “almost as good as seven” is dangerously wrong: at five shuffles, total variation remains above 0.90.
  • Floating-point overflow and underflow. Computing \(\binom{2^k + n - r}{n}\) and \(2^{kn}\) for \(n=52\) involves numbers with dozens of digits (for \(k=7\), \(2^{kn} = 2^{364} \approx 3.8 \times 10^{109}\)). Attempting this calculation with standard 64-bit IEEE floats immediately overflows to inf. Python’s arbitrary-precision integers and fractions.Fraction are required to maintain exact arithmetic before converting to float for display.
  • Ignoring non-reachability. For small \(k\), \(2^k < n\), so permutations with \(r > 2^k\) have probability exactly zero. Python’s math.comb(a, b) already returns 0 when \(b > a\). A hand-rolled binomial built from factorials or the Gamma function may not, and can produce garbage terms.

9. Exercises

  1. The Identity Cut Distribution. Prove algebraically that after 1 shuffle (\(k=1\)), the Bayer–Diaconis formula gives \(P_1(\text{id}) = (n + 1)/2^n\). Explain why each of the \(n+1\) cut points \(c \in \{0, 1, \dots, n\}\) can generate the identity permutation.
  2. Rising Sequences of the Reversal. Let \(\pi_{\text{rev}} = (n, n-1, \dots, 2, 1)\) be the permutation that completely reverses the deck. How many rising sequences does \(\pi_{\text{rev}}\) have? Using the condition \(2^k \ge r\), what is the minimum number of GSR shuffles required before \(\pi_{\text{rev}}\) has a non-zero probability of appearing?
  3. Alternative Deck Sizes. Modify tv_table.py to compute the total variation distance for a 32-card deck (used in Piquet and Skat). Identify the exact shuffle \(k\) at which the total variation distance first drops below \(0.5\), and compare it against the theoretical cutoff \((3/2)\log_2(32)\).

Summary

The Gilbert–Shannon–Reeds model provides a mathematically tractable and physically realistic framework for riffle shuffles. By identifying rising sequences and mapping them to the Eulerian numbers of inverse permutations, Bayer and Diaconis unlocked an exact closed-form distribution for a deck of cards after \(k\) shuffles. Rather than randomizing incrementally, card decks undergo a sharp cutoff at \((3/2)\log_2 n\). Recent work shows that sloppier cuts keep the cutoff but move it later. For a standard 52-card deck, the deck remains largely unmixed through five shuffles and crosses the threshold of randomness at seven.
